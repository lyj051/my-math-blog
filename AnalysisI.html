Theorem 1: Stone-Weierstrass theorem

Let \(\mathcal{A}\) be an algebra of real continuous functions on a compact set \(K\subset \R^n\). If \(\mathcal{A}\) separates points on \(K\) and if \(\mathcal{A}\) vanishes at no point of \(K\), then the uniform closure \(\mathcal{B}\) of \(\mathcal{A}\) consists of all real continuous functions on \(K\).

Integration

Definition 1

Suppose that \(I^k\) is a \(k\)-cell in \(\R^k\) consisting of all \[ \x=(x_1,\cdots,x_k) \] such that \[ a_i \leq x_i \leq b_i \qquad i=1,\ldots,k \] \(I^j\) is the \(j\)-cell in \(\R^j\) defined by the first \(j\) inequalities, and \(f\) is a real continuous function on \(I^k\).

Put \(f=f_k\) and define \(f_{k-1}\) on \(I^{k-1}\) by \[ f_{k-1}(x_1,\cdots,x_{k-1})=\int _{a_k} ^{b_k} f_k(x_1,\cdots,x_{k-1},x_k)\d x_k \] The uniform continuity of \(f_k\) on \(I^k\) shows that \(f_{k-1}\) is continuous on \(I^{k-1}\). Hence we can repeat this process and obtain functions \(f_j\), contiunous on \(I^j\), such that \(f_{j-1}\) is the integral of \(f_j\) with respect to \(x_j\) over \([a_j,b_j]\). After \(k\) steps we arrive at a number \(f_0\), which we call the integral of \(f\) over \(I^k\); we write it in the form \[ \int_{I^k} f(\x)\d\x \,. \]

A priori, this definition of the integral depends on the order in which the \(k\) integrations are carried out. To prove thiss, let us introduce the temporary notation \(L(f)\) for the integral and \(L^\prime(f)\) for the result obtained by carring out the \(k\) integrations in some other order.

Lemma 2

For every \(f\in C(I^k)\), \(L(f)=L^\prime(f)\).

Proof. If \(h(\x)=h_1(x_1)\cdots h_k(x_k)\), where \(h_j\in C[a_j,b_j]\), then \[ L(h)=\prod_{i=1}^k \int _{a_i}^{b_i}h_i(x_i)\d x_i=L^{\prime}(h). \] If \(\mathcal{A}\) is the set of all finite sums of such functions \(h\), it follows that \(L(g)=L^\prime(g)\) for all \(g\in\mathcal{A}\). Also, \(\mathcal{A}\) is an algebra of functions on \(I^k\) to which the Stone-Weierstrass Theorem applies.

Put \(V=\prod_{i=1}^k(b_i-a_i)\). If \(f\in C(I^k)\) and \(\epsilon >0\), there exists \(g\in \mathcal{A}\) such that \(\Vert f-g\Vert < \epsilon/V\), where \(\Vert f \Vert\) is defined as \(\max \vert f(\x) \vert\,(\x \in I^k)\). Then \(\vert L(f-g)\vert<\epsilon\), \(\vert L^\prime(f-g)\vert < \epsilon\), and since \[ L(f)-L^\prime(f)=L(f-g)+L^\prime(g-f) \] we conclude that \(\vert L(f)-L^\prime (f) \vert <2\epsilon\).

Definition 2

The support of a (real or complex) function \(f\) on \(\R^k\) is the closure of the set of all points \(\x \in \R^k\) at which \(f(\x)\neq 0\). If \(f\) is a continuous function with compact support, let \(I^k\) be any \(k\)-cell which contains the support of \(f\) and define \[ \int _{\R^k}f:=\int_{I^k}f\,. \] The integral so defined is evidently independent of the choice of \(I^k\), provided only that \(I^k\) ccontains the support of \(f\).

Primitive mappings

Definition 3

If \(\mathbf{G}\) maps an open set \(E\subset \mathbf{R}^n\) into \(\mathbf{R}^n\), and if there is an integer \(m\) and a real function \(g\) with domain \(E\) such that \[ \begin{equation} \mathbf{G}(\x)=\sum_{i\neq m} x_i \mathbf{e}_i+g(\x)\mathbf{e}_m , \qquad x\in E \end{equation} \tag{1}\] then we call \(\mathbf{G}\) primitive.

A primitive mapping is thus one that changes at most one coordinate; If \(g\) is differentiable at some point \(\aa\in E\), so is \(\G\). The matrix \([\alpha_{ij}]\) of the operator \((\d\G)_{\aa}\) has \[ [(\frac{\partial g}{\partial x_1})_{\aa},\cdots,(\frac{\partial g}{\partial x_m})_{\aa},\cdots,(\frac{\partial g}{\partial x_n})_{\aa}] \] as its \(m\)’th row. For \(j\neq m\), we have \(\alpha_{jj}=1\) and \(\alpha_{ji}=0\) if \(i\neq j\). The Jacobian of \(\G\) at \(\aa\) is thus given by \[ J_G(\aa)=\det[(\d \G)_{\aa}]=(\frac{\partial g}{\partial x_m})_{\aa}\,. \] Clearly \((\d\G)_{\aa}\) is invertible if and only if \((\frac{\partial g}{\partial x_m})_{\aa}\neq 0\).

Notation 1

  1. A linear operator \(B\) on \(\R^n\) that interchanges some pair members of the standard basis and leaves the others fixed is called a flip.

  2. By \(P_m,0\leq m\leq n\) we mean the projections in \(\R^n\): \(P_0 \x=0\) and \[ P_m \x=x_1\mathbf{e}_1+\cdots+x_m\mathbf{e}_m,\qquad x\in\R^n\,. \]

Theorem 3: Theorem of local representation

Suppose \(\mathbf{F}\) is a \(C^1\)-mapping of an open set \(E\subset \R^n\) into \(\R^n\), \(\mathbf{0}\in E\), \(\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{0})=\mathbf{0}\), and \((\d\mathbf{F})_{\mathbf{0}}\) is invertible. Then there is a neighbourhood of \(\mathbf{0}\) in \(\R^n\) in which a representation \[ \begin{equation} \mathbf{F}(\x)=B_1\circ\cdots\circ B_{n-1}\circ \G_n \circ \cdots \circ \mathbf{G}_1(\x) \end{equation} \tag{2}\] is valid.

In Equation 2, each \(\G_i\) is a primitive \(C^1\)-mapping in some neighborhood of \(\mathbf{0}\); \(\G_i(\mathbf{0})=\mathbf{0}\), \((\d\G_i)_0\) is invertible, and each \(B_i\) is either a flip or the identity operator.

Briefly, Equation 2 represents \(\mathbf{F}\) locally as a composition of primitive mappings and flips.

Proof. Assume that for each \(1\leq m\leq n-1\) there exists a neighborhood \(V_m\) of \(\0\) and a \(C^1\)-mapping \(\F_m\) on it, with \(\F_m(\0)=\0\) and \((\d\F_m)_0\) invertible, for which the following holds: \[ P_{m-1}\F_m(\x)=P_{m-1}\x\qquad (\x\in V_m). \tag{3}\]

By Equation 3, we have \[ \F_m(\x)=P_{m-1}\x+\sum_{i=m}^{n}\alpha_i(\x)\mathbf{e}_i, \] where \(\alpha_m,\dots,\alpha_n\) are real \(C^1\)-functions in \(V_m\). Hence \[ (\d\F_m)_\0\, \mathbf{e}_m=\sum_{i=m}^{n}(\frac{\partial \alpha_i}{\partial x_m})_\0\, \mathbf{e}_i \tag{4}\] Since \((\d\F_m)_\0\) is invertible, the left side of Equation 4 is not \(\0\), and therefore there is a \(k\) such that \(m\leq k \leq n\) and \((\frac{\partial \alpha_k}{\partial x_m})_\0\neq 0\).

Let \(B_m\) be the flip that interchanges this \(m\) and this \(k\) (if \(k=m\), \(B_m\) is the identity) and define \[ \G_m(\x)=\x+[\alpha_k(\x)-x_m]\mathbf{e}_m \qquad \x\in V_m\,. \] Then \(\G_m\in C^1(V_m)\), \(\G_m\) is primitive and \((\d\G_m)_\0\) is invertible.

The inverse function theorem shows therefore that there is an open set \(U_m\) with \(\0\in U_m\subset V_m\) such that \(\G_m\) is a 1-1 mapping of \(U_m\) onto a neighborhood \(V_{m+1}\) of \(\0\), in which \(\G^{-1}_m\) is continuously differentiable. Define \(\F_{m+1}\) by \[ \F_{m+1}(\y)=B_m\circ \F_m \circ \G_m^{-1} (\y),\qquad \y\in V_{m+1}\,. \tag{5}\] Then \(\F_{m+1}\in C^1(V_{m+1}),\F_{m+1}(\0)=\0\) and \((\d\F_{m+1})_\0\) is invertible by the chain rule. Also, for \(\x\in U_m\), \[ \begin{aligned} P_m\F_{m+1}(\G_m(\x)) & = P_m B_m \circ \F_m(\x) \\ & = P_m[P_{m+1}\x+\alpha_k(\x)\mathbf{e}_m+\cdots] \\ & =P_{m-1}\x+\alpha_k(\x)\mathbf{e}_m \\ &=P_m \G_m (\x) \end{aligned} \] so that \[ P_m\F_{m+1}(\y)=P_m\y, \qquad \y \in V_{m+1}\,. \] Take \(\F_1=\F\); it follows from the argument above that Equation 3 also holds for \(m=n\). Moreover, since \(B_m\circ B_m=I\), Equation 5, with \(\y=\G_m(\x)\), is equivalent to \[ \F_m(\x)=B_m\circ \F_{m+1} (\G_m(\x)), \qquad \x\in U_m\,. \] If we apply this with \(m=1,\ldots,n-1\), we successively obtain \[ \begin{aligned} \F=\F_1 & =B_1\circ \F_2 \circ \G_1 \\ &=B_1\circ B_2 \circ \F_3 \circ \G_2 \circ \G_1 = \cdots \\ &=B_1\circ \cdots \circ B_{n-1} \circ \F_n \circ \G_{n-1} \circ \cdots \circ \G_1 \end{aligned} \] in some neighborhood of \(\0\). By Equation 3, \(\F_n\) is primitive. This completes the proof.

Partitions of Unity

Theorem 4

Suppose that \(K\) is a compact subset of \(\mathbf{R}^n\) and \(\{V_\alpha\}\) is an open cover of \(K\). Then there exist functions \(\psi_1,\dots, \psi_s\in C(\R^n)\) such that

  1. \(0\leq \psi_i \leq 1\) for \(1\leq i\leq s\);

  2. each \(\psi_i\) has its support in some \(V_\alpha\), and

  3. \(\psi_1(\x)+\dots+\psi_s(\x)=1\) for every \(\x \in K\).

\(\{\psi_i\}\) is called a partition of unity, and (b) is sometimes expressed by saying that \(\{\psi_i\}\) is subordinate to the cover \(\{V_\alpha\}\).

Corollary 5

If \(f\in C(\R^n)\) and the support of \(f\) lies in \(K\), then \[ f=\sum_{i=1}^s \psi_i f\,. \] Each \(\psi_if\) has its support in some \(V_\alpha\).

Change of variables in multiple integrals

Theorem 6

Suppose \(T\) is a one-to-one \(C^1\)-mapping of an open set \(E\subset \R^k\) into \(\R^k\) such that \(J_T(\x)\neq 0\) for all \(\x \in E\). If \(f\) is a continuous function on \(\R^k\) whose support is compact and lies in \(T(E)\), then \[ \int_{\R^k} f(\y) \d\y = \int_{\R^k} f(T(\x)) \, |J_T(\x)| \d\x. \tag{6}\]

Proof. \(\textbf{step 1}\): If \(T\) is a primitive mapping, then \(T\) only changes one coordinate, say \(T(\x)=(x_1,\dots,\hat{x_t},\dots,x_k)\) where \(\hat{x_t}=g(\x)\). It follows from the change of variable in integral of one variable that \[ \begin{aligned} \int _{\R^k}f(T(\x)) \, \vert J_T(\x)\vert \d\x & =\int_{\R^k}f(x_1,\dots,\hat{x_t},\dots,x_k)\,\big\vert \frac{\partial g}{\partial x_t}\big\vert\d\x \\ & = \int _{\R^{k-1}}\d\x\int _{\R}f(x_1,\dots,\hat{x_t},\dots,x_k)\big\vert\frac{\partial g}{\partial x_t}\big\vert \d \x_t \\ & = \int _{\R^{k-1}} \d\x \int_{\R} f(x_1,\dots,x_t,\dots,x_n)\d\x_t \\ & = \int _{\R^k} f(\x) \d\x\,. \end{aligned} \] Thus Equation 6 holds for primitive mappings.

\(\textbf{step 2}\): If \(T\) is a flip which interchanges two coordinates, say \(x_m\) and \(x_n\), then Equation 6 also holds, for Lemma 2 and \[ \int_{\R^k} f(T(\x)) \, \vert J_T(\x) \vert \d\x = \int _{\R^k} f(T\x)\d\x\,. \]

\(\textbf{step 3}\): If Equation 6 holds for \(T\) and \(Q\), then it also holds for \(S=T\circ Q\). Because: \[ J_{T\circ Q}(\x)=J_T(\x) \cdot J_Q(\x). \]

\(\textbf{step 4}\): Each point \(\mathbf{a} \in E\) has a neighborhood \(U\subset E\) in which \[ T(\x)=T(\mathbf{a})+B_1\cdots B_{k-1}\G_k \circ \G_{k-1}\circ \cdots \circ \G_1 (\x -\mathbf{a}), \] where \(\G_i\) and \(B_i\) are as in Equation 2. Setting \(V=T(U)\), it follows that Equation 6 holds if the support of \(f\) lies in \(V\). Thus:

Each point \(\y\in T(E)\) lies in an open set \(V_{\y}\subset T(E)\) such that Equation 6 holds for all continuous functions whose support lies in \(V_{\y}\).

\(\textbf{step 5}\): Now let \(f\) be a continuous function with compact support \(K\subset T(E)\). Since \(\{V_{\y}\}\) covers \(K\), the Corollary of partition of unity shows that \(f=\sum \psi_i f\), where each \(\psi_i\) is continuous and each \(\psi_i\) has its support in some \(V_{\y}\). Thus Equation 6 holds for each \(\psi_i f\), and hence also for their sum \(f\).
