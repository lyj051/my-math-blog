Theorem 1: Weierstrass’s Theorem

Suppose that the terms in the series \(\sum_1^\infty f_n\left(z\right)\) are single-valued functions analytic in a domain \(G\) and that this series converges uniformly in every closed circle 1 belonging to \(G\). Then

  1. the sum \(f(z)\) of the series is analytic in this domain,；

  2. the series can be differentiated termwise any number of times, i.e. \(\sum _1^{\infty}f_n^{(k)}(z) = f^{(k)}(z)\)；

  3. all the series obtained as a result of such differentiation converge uniformly in every closed circle belonging to \(G\).

In what follows, for the sake of brevity we will call a series that converges in every circle belonging to a domain \(G\) uniformly convergent inside domain \(G\). Using the Heine-Borel theorem on coverings, we can easily prove that uniform convergence inside a domain \(G\) is equivalent to uniform convergence on every closed, bounded point set belonging to \(G\). Note that uniform convergence inside \(G\) does not require that the series be convergent in the entire domain \(G\). For instance,

Example 1

Consider the function \(f_n(z)=z^n\) on the open unit disk \(\mathbb{E}=\{z:|z| < 1\}\).

Inside any closed circle \(K\) belonging to \(\mathbb{E}\), it follows that \(|z| \leq q < 1\). Thus \(\sup_{z \in K}|f_n(z)|=|z|^n \leq q^n \to 0\). Then \(f_n(z)\) is uniformly convergent inside \(\mathbb{E}\).

However for any integer \(N\), we have \(\sup_{z\in\mathbb{E}}|f_n(z)-0|=1\) and then \(f_n(z)\) does not converge to \(0\) in the entire disk.[another example]

For the theorem to be valid it is essential that its hypothesis be satisfed for a domain in the plane. More precisely, it is essential that for each point of \(G\) there exists a circle with its center at that point and lying inside \(G\). For instance, if we consider a series whose terms are the simplest analytic functions, polynomials \(P_n(z)\) that uniformly converge in a segment of the real axis (this segment constitutes a set of points for which the hypothesis is not satisfed), the sum of such a series may be not differentiable in the segment, even if it is, we may not be able to justify termwise differentiation of the series.* Therefore, Weierstrass’s theorem expresses a specific property of analytic functions of a complex variable.

Proof. We start the proof by noting that it suffices to prove the theorem for an arbitrary circle \(K: | z- z_0\| < \rho\) that helongs to \(G\) together with its interior. To prove proposition (a) we use Morera’s theorem. Since the series converges uniformly in \(\overline{K}\) and its terms are continuous, its sum \(f(z)\) is also continuous in \(K\) and the series can be termwise integrated along any triangular contour \(\triangle\) lying in \(K\). Whence \[ \int_{\triangle} f \operatorname{d}\!z = \sum_{1}^{\infty} \int_{\triangle} f_n \operatorname{d}\!z = 0\,. \] (we have employed the fact that the \(f_n(z)\) is analytic in \(K\).) From Morera’s theorem it then follows that \(f(z)\) is analytic in \(K\) and, hence, it is analytic at all points of \(G\).

Next, if \(z\in K\), then \[ f^{(k)}(z)= \frac{k!}{2\pi i} \int _{|\zeta-z_0|=\rho} \frac{f(\zeta)}{(\zeta -z)^{k+1}} \operatorname{d}\!\zeta \] But \(\frac{f(\zeta)}{(\zeta-z)^{k+1}}=\sum_{1}^{\infty}\frac{f_n(\zeta)}{(\zeta-z)^{k+1}}\) where the last series is uniformly convergent for a fixed \(z\) on the circle \(|\zeta -z_0|=\rho\) (by Abel’s criterion). We can, therefore perform termwise integration with respect to \(\zeta\) and, hence, \[ \begin{aligned} f^{(k)}\left(z\right)=&\frac{k!}{2\pi i}\int_{|\zeta-z|=\rho}\sum_{1}^{\infty}\frac{f_{n}\left(\zeta\right)}{(\zeta-z)^{k+1}}d\zeta\\&=\sum_{1}^{\infty}\frac{k!}{2\pi i}\int_{|\zeta-z_{0}|=\rho}\frac{f_{n}\left(\zeta\right)d\zeta}{(\zeta-z)^{n+1}}=\sum_{1}^{\infty}f_{n}^{(k)}\left(z\right), \end{aligned} \] i.e., we have shown that proposition (b) is valid, too.

Finally, to prove proposition (c), consider a circle \(K^\prime:|z-z_0|<\rho^\prime\), where \(\rho^\prime\) is greater than \(\rho\) but smaller than the distance from \(z_0\) to the boundary of \(G\). The closed circle \(\overline{K}\) belongs to \(G\) and contains the closed circle \(\overline{K}\). For each point \(z\in\overline{K}\) we have \[ \begin{aligned} \left|f^{(k)}\left(z\right)-\sum_{1}^{N}f_{n}^{(k)}\left(z\right)\right|&=\left|\frac{k!}{2\pi i}\int_{|\zeta-z_{i}|=p^{\prime}}\frac{f\left(\zeta\right)d\zeta}{\left(\zeta-z\right)^{k+1}}-\sum_{1}^{N}\frac{k!}{2\pi i}\int_{|\zeta-z_{i}|=n^{\prime}}\frac{f_{n}\left(\zeta\right)d\zeta}{\left(\zeta-z\right)^{k+1}}\right|\\&\leqslant\frac{k!}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{\left|f\left(\zeta\right)-\sum_{1}^{N}f_{n}\left(\zeta\right)\right|}{\left|\zeta-z\right|^{k+1}}\varphi^{\prime}\:d\varphi\\&<\frac{k!}{2\pi}\frac{2\pi\rho^{\prime}}{(\rho^{\prime}-\rho)^{k+1}}\sup_{|\zeta-z_{0}|=\rho^{\prime}}\left|f\left(\zeta\right)-\sum_{1}^{N}f_{n}\left(\zeta\right)\right|. \end{aligned} \]

In view of the uniform convergence of \(\sum_1^{\infty}f_{n}\left(\zeta\right)\) on the circle \(|\zeta-z_0|=\rho^{\prime}\) we can make the last factor arbitrarily small provided that \(N\) is suffciently large (regardless of the position of \(z\in\overline{K}\).) Hence, for any \(\epsilon>0\) and \(z\in \overline{K}\) we have

\[ |f^{(k)}(z)-\sum_{1}^{N}f_{n}^{(k)}(z)|<\epsilon\quad \text{if }:N>N_{0}(\epsilon). \]

This completes the proof of Weierstrass’s theorem.

[another example]: c.f. remark of Theorem 1
