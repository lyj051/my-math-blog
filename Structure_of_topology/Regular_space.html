regular space and other spaces

Authors

R.Engelking

N.Bourbaki

Published

October 14, 2026

Prerequisites

Base and subbase

A topological space \(X\) is called a regular space if \(X\) is a \(T_1\)-space1 and for every \(x\in X\) and every closed set \(F\subset X\) such that \(x\notin F\) there exist open sets \(U_1,U_2\) such that \(x\in U_1,F\subset U_2\) and \(U_1\cap U_2=\emptyset\).

Clearly the separation condition is equivalent to the following Characterization:

Characterization 1: \((\mathbf{O}_{\rm{III}})\)

Each point \(x\in X\) has a closed neighborhood basis.

Proposition 1

A \(T_1\)-space \(X\) is a regular space if and only if for every \(x\in X\) and every neighborhood \(V\) of \(x\) in a fixed subbase \(\mathscr{P}\) there exists a neighborhood \(U\) of \(x\) such that \(\overline{U}\subset V\).

In particular, a \(T_1\)-space \(X\) is a regular space if and only if for every \(x\in X\) and every neighborhood \(V\) of \(x\) there exists a neighborhood \(U\) of \(x\) such that \(\overline{U}\subset V\).

Proof. Suppose that \(X\) is a regular space and \(\mathscr{P}\) a subbase for \(X\); take an \(x\in X\) and a neighborhood \(V\in \mathscr{P}\) of \(x\). By the definition of a regular space, there exist open sets \(U_1,U_2\) such that \(x\in U_1\) and \(X\setminus V \subset U_2\) and \(U_1\cap U_2=\emptyset\). We then have \(U_1\subset X\setminus U_2 \subset V\) which implies that \(\overline{U_1}\subset V\), since \(X\setminus U_2\) is closed.

Suppose now that the condition is satisfied. Take an \(x\in X\) and a closed set \(F\) such that \(x\notin F\). By the definiton of a subbase there exist \(V_1,\dots,V_k\in \mathscr{P}\) satisfying \(x\in \bigcap_{i=1}^{k} V_i \subset X\setminus F\). For \(i=1,\dots,k\) take a neighborhood \(W_i\) of \(x\) such that \(\overline{W_i}\subset V_i\). The open sets \(U_1=\bigcap_{i=1}^{k}W_i\) and \(U_2=X\setminus \bigcap_{i=1}^{k} \overline{W_i}\) are disjoint, \(x\in U_1\) and \[ F \subset X\setminus \bigcap_{i=1}^{k}V_i \subset X\setminus \bigcap_{i=1}^{k} \overline{W_i}=U_2\,. \]

We shall now give an example of a Hausdorff space that is not regular.

Example 2: T_2 but not T_3

Let \(X\) be the set of real numbers and \(Z\subset X\) the set of reciprocals of all integers different from zero. For every \(x\in X\) let \(U_i(x)=(x-1/i,x+1/i)\) and \[ B(x)= \begin{cases} \{U_i(x)\}_{i=1}^{\infty} \quad \text{if } x\neq 0\,,\\ \{U_i(x) \setminus Z\}_{i=1}^{\infty} \quad \text{if }x=0\,. \end{cases} \]

Proposition 2
  • Any subsapce of a regular topological space \(X\) is regular.
  • If for any point \(x\in X\) there exists a regular closed neighborhood of \(x\), then \(X\) is regular.
Example 3: T_1 but not T_2

Suppose that \(X\) is the interval \([-1,+1]\) of \(\mathbf{R}\). We consider the equivalence relation \(S\) on \(X\): if \(x\neq \pm 1\), the equivalence class of \(x\) is \(\{x,-x\}\); the equivalence class of \(1\) and \(-1\) are each reduced to that point. Show that the relation \(S\) is open and that the quotient space \(X/S\) is accessible (\(T_1\)) but not separated (\(T_2\)). Furthermore, every point of \(X/S\) possesses a neighborhood in \(X/S\) which is a regular subspace.

Exercise 1
  1. In a topological space \(X\), an open set \(U\) is said to be regular if we have \(U=\operatorname{int}(\overline{U})\). It is equivalent to saying that \(U\) is the interior of some closed set. \(X\) is semi-regular (the topology \(\mathscr{T}\) on it is then called semi-regular) if the regular open sets in \(X\) form a base of \(X\). Prove that if \(\mathscr{T}\) satisfies \((\mathbf{O}_{\rm{III}})\), then \(\mathscr{T}\) is semi-regular.

Footnotes

  1. Clearly, every regular space is a Hausdorff space. It is in order to guarantee this implication that we assume - besides the possibility of separation of points and closed sets - that a regular space is a \(T_1\)-space. The anti-discrete space has the required property of separation but it is not a \(T_1\)-space.↩︎
