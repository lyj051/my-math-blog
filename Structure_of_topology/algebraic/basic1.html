Functors

Example 1

There is a contravariant functor \(C\) from the category of compact Hausdorff spaces and continuous maps to the category of normed rings over \(\mathbb{R}\) and continuous homomorphisms which assigns to \(X\) its normed ring of continuous real-valued functions.

Remark. The norm is defined to be \(\Vert f \Vert = \sup _{x\in X} \vert f(x) \vert\) where \(f\in C(X)=C(X;\mathbb{R})\).

Example 2

There is a covariant functor \(H_0\) from the category of topological spaces and continuous maps to the category of abelian groups and homomorphisms such that \(H_0(X)\) is the free abelian group generated by the set of components of \(X\),and if \(f{:}X\to Y\),then \(H_0(f){:}H_0({X})\to H_0(Y)\) is the homomorphism such that if \(C\) is a component of \(X\) and \(C^\prime\) is the component of \(Y\) containing \(f(C)\), then \(H_0(f) C= C^\prime\).

Any functor from the category of topological spaces and continuous maps to an algebraic category (such as the category of abelian groups and homomorphisms) is a representation of the topological category by an algebraic one. Algebraic topology is the study of such functors; we show that simple remarks about functors can be used to obtain necessary conditions for the solvability of topological problems.

Theorem 1

Let \(T\) be a functor from a category \(\mathcal{C}\) to a category \(\mathcal{D}\). Then \(T\) maps isomorphism in \(\mathcal{C}\) to isomorphism in \(\mathcal{D}\).

In particular, if \(T\) is an algebraic functor on the category of topological spaces and continuous maps, a necessary condition that \(X\) be homeomorphic to \(Y\) is that \(T(X)\cong T(Y)\).

Example 3

The real line \(\mathbb{R}\) and the real plane \(\mathbb{R}^2\) are not homeomorphic.

If they were homeomorphic, then \(\mathbb{R}-0\) would be homeomorphic to \(\mathbb{R}^2-p\) for some \(p\in\mathbb{R}^2\), but \(H_0(\mathbb{R}-0)\) is a free abelian group on two generators, while \(H_0({\mathbb{R}}^2-p)\) is a free abelian group on one generator.

In applications of algebraic functors to topological problems the algebra will frequently play an essential role. For example, let \(T_0(X)\) be the functor obtained by composing the functor \(H_0\) with the forgetful functor, which assigns to every abelian group its underlying set. The functor \(T_0\) contains less information than the functor \(H_0\) and does not give as strong a necessary condition for homeomorphism, for example, \(T_0( \mathbb{R} - 0)\) and \(T_0(\mathbb{R}^2-p)\) are both countably infı nite sets and are equivalent in the category of sets and functions. For this reason it is important to provide functors with as much algebraic structure as possible. Later we shall consider functors which depend on a chosen topological space. These functors take values in the category of sets and functions, but sonne of them, depending on properties of the particular spaces which define them, are functors to the category of groups and homomorphisms. The added algebraic structure in such cases will prove useful.

To show how functors can be applied to another problem, let \(A\) be a subspace of a topological space \(X\) and let \(f:A\rightarrow Y\) be continuous. The extension problem is to determine whether \(f\) has a continuous extension to \(X\)一that is, whether the dotted arrow in the triangle.

Theorem 2

Let \(T\) be a covariant functor (or contravariant functor) from the category of topological spaces and continuous maps to a category \(\mathcal{C}\). A necessary condition that a map \(f: A \to Y\) be extendable to \(X\) (where \(i: A \subset X\)) is that there exists a morphism \(\varphi: T(X) \to T(Y)\) [or \(\varphi: T(Y) \to T(X)\)] such that \[ \varphi \circ T(i) = T(f) \quad [\text{or } T(f) = T(i) \circ \varphi]. \]

Proof. Assume that \(f': X \to Y\) is an extension of \(f\). Then \(f'\circ i = f\). Therefore

\[ T(f') \circ T(i) = T(f) \quad [\text{or } T(f) = T(i) \circ T(f')], \]

and \(T(f')\) can be taken as the morphism \(\varphi\).

Example 4

The above result can be applied to prove that the identity map of \(\dot{I}\) cannot be extended to a continuous map \(I \to \dot{I}\). We use the functor \(H_0\) and obtain the necessary condition that there must exist a homomorphism \(\varphi: H_0(I) \to H_0(\dot{I})\) such that \(\varphi \circ H_0(i) = H_0(1_{\dot{I}})\) (where \(i: \dot{I} \subset I\)). Because \(H_0(\dot{I})\) is a free abelian group on two generators and \(H_0(I)\) is a free abelian group on one generator, there is no such homomorphism \(\varphi\). Again, this is a trivial example, but it illustrates the method, and the general homology functors \(H_q\) defined later can be used in the same way to show that there is no continuous map \(E^{n+1} \to S^n\) that is the identity map on \(S^n\).

Remark. Here \(I=[0,1]\) and \(\dot{I}=\{0,1\}=S^0\).

Thus we see that a functor yields necessary conditions for the solvability of topological problems. There are situations in which these necessary conditions are also sufficient. For example, the functor \(C\) of example 1 gives a necessary and sufficient condition for homeomorphism—that is, two compact Hausdorff spaces \(X\) and \(Y\) are homeomorphic if and only if \(C(X)\) and \(C(Y)\) are isomorphic. This is not a particularly useful result, however, because it seems to be no easier to determine whether or not two normed rings are isomorphic than it is to determine whether or not two compact Hausdorff spaces are homeomorphic. We seek functors to categories that are somewhat simpler than the category of topological spaces, so that the algebraic problems that arise in these categories can be effectively solved. One big problem of algebraic topology is to find, and compute, sufficiently many such functors that the solvability of a particular topological problem is equivalent to the solvability of the corresponding (and simpler) algebraic problems.

Homotopy

The problem of classifying topological spaces and continuous maps up to homeomorphism does not seem to be amenable to attack directly by computable algebraic functors, as described in Functors. Many of the computable functors, because they are computable, are invariant under continuous deformation. Therefore they cannot distinguish between spaces (or maps) that can be continuously deformed from one to the other; the most that can be hoped for from such functors is that they characterize the space (or map) up to continuous deformation.

The intuitive concept of a continuous deformation will be made precise in this section in the concept of homotopy. This leads to the homotopy category which is fundamental for algebraic topology. Its objects are topological spaces and its morphisms are equivalence classes of continuous maps (two maps being equivalent if one can be continuously deformed into the other).For technical reasons we consider not just the homotopy category of topological spaces, but rather the larger homotopy category of pairs.

A topological pair \((X,A)\) consists of a topological space \(X\) and a subspace \(A \subset X\). If \(A\) is empty, denoted by \(\varnothing\), we shall not distinguish between the pair \((X,\varnothing)\) and the space \(X\). A subpair \((X',A') \subset (X,A)\) consists of a pair with \(X' \subset X\) and \(A' \subset A\). A map \(f: (X,A) \to (Y,B)\) between pairs is a continuous function \(f\) from \(X\) to \(Y\) such that \(f(A) \subset B\), and there is a category of topological pairs and maps between them which contains as full subcategories the category of topological spaces and continuous maps, as well as the category of pointed topological spaces and continuous maps.

NoteExplanation: Full Subcategories

1. What is a “full subcategory”? A subcategory is “full” if it contains all the morphisms between its objects that exist in the larger category. In other words, it selects a subset of objects but keeps every single map between them.

2. The Two Full Subcategories: * The category of topological spaces and continuous maps: By setting \(A = \varnothing\), the topological pair \((X, \varnothing)\) is identified with the ordinary space \(X\). Any continuous map \(f: X \to Y\) automatically satisfies \(f(\varnothing) \subset B\). Therefore, the entire category of topological spaces is perfectly embedded as a full subcategory. * The category of pointed topological spaces and continuous maps: By setting \(A = \{x_0\}\) (a single point), the pair \((X, \{x_0\})\) represents a pointed space. The mapping condition \(f(\{x_0\}) \subset \{y_0\}\) means \(f(x_0) = y_0\), which is exactly the definition of a basepoint-preserving continuous map. Thus, the category of pointed spaces is also a full subcategory.

Given a pair \((X,A)\), we let \((X,A) \times I\) denote the pair \((X \times I, A \times I)\). Let \(X' \subset X\) and suppose that \(f_0, f_1: (X,A) \to (Y,B)\) agree on \(X'\) (that is, \(f_0|X' = f_1|X'\)). Then \(f_0\) is homotopic to \(f_1\) relative to \(X'\), denoted by \(f_0 \simeq f_1\) rel \(X'\), if there exists a map

\[ F: (X,A) \times I \to (Y,B) \]

such that \(F(x,0) = f_0(x)\) and \(F(x,1) = f_1(x)\) for \(x \in X\) and \(F(x,t) = f_0(x)\) for \(x \in X'\) and \(t \in I\). Such a map \(F\) is called a homotopy relative to \(X'\) from \(f_0\) to \(f_1\) and is denoted by \(F: f_0 \simeq f_1\) rel \(X'\). If \(X' = \varnothing\), we omit the phrase “relative to \(\varnothing\).” Clearly, \(f_0 \simeq f_1\) rel \(X'\) implies \(f_0 \simeq f_1\) rel \(X''\) for any \(X'' \subset X'\). A map from \(X\) to \(Y\) is said to be null homotopic, or inessential, if it is homotopic to some constant map.

For \(t \in I\) define \(h_t: (X,A) \to (X,A) \times I\) by \(h_t(x) = (x,t)\). If \(F: f_0 \simeq f_1\) rel \(X'\), then \(Fh_0 = f_0\), \(Fh_1 = f_1\), and \(Fh_t|X' = f_0|X'\) for all \(t \in I\). Therefore the collection \(\{Fh_t\}_{t \in I}\) is a continuous one-parameter family of maps from \((X,A)\) to \((Y,B)\), agreeing on \(X'\), which connects \(f_0 = Fh_0\) to \(f_1 = Fh_1\). Hence \(f_0 \simeq f_1\) rel \(X'\) corresponds to the intuitive idea of continuously deforming \(f_0\) into \(f_1\) by maps all of which agree on \(X'\). Note that if \(f_0 \simeq f_1\) rel \(X'\) there will usually be many maps \(F\) which are homotopies relative to \(X'\) from \(f_0\) to \(f_1\).

Contractible space

The simplest nonempty space is a one-point space. We characterize the homotopy type of such a space as follows. A topological space \(X\) is said to be contractible if the identity map of \(X\) is homotopic to some constant map of \(X\) to itself. A homotopy from \(\operatorname{id}_X\) to the constant map of \(X\) to \(x_0 \in X\) is called a contraction of \(X\) to \(x_0\).

Lemma 3

Any two maps of an arbitrary space to a contractible space are homotopic.

Corollary 4

If \(Y\) is contractible, any two constant maps of \(Y\) to itself are homotopic, and the identity map is homotopic to any constant map of \(Y\) to itself.

It is interesting to observe that lemma 3 cannot be strengthened to the case of relative homotopy. That is, if \(f_0\) and \(f_1\) are maps of \(X\) into a contractible space \(Y\) which agree on \(X^\prime \subset X\), it need not be true that \(f_0 \simeq f_1\) rel \(X^\prime\). The comb space illustrates this.

Example 5

Let \(Y\) be comb space. Let \(F:Y\times I \rightarrow Y\) be defined by \(F \big((x,y),t \big)=(x,(1-t)y)\). Then \(F\) is a homotopy from \(\operatorname{id}_Y\) to the projection of \(Y\) to the \(x\)-axis. The latter is homotopic to a constant map \(Y\rightarrow \{0\}\) via \(F\big((x,y),t\big)=((1-t)x,0)\). Thus \(Y\) is contractible. Let \(c:Y \rightarrow Y\) be the constant map of \(Y\) to the point \((0,1)\). By corollary 4, it follows that \(\operatorname{id}_Y \simeq c\), but even though these two maps agree on \((0,1)\), there is no homotopy relative to \((0,1)\) between them.

Proof. Suppose there was a homotopy \(H:Y \times I \rightarrow Y\) such that \[ H\big((x,y),0\big)=(x,y), \quad H\big((x,y),1\big)=(0,1), \quad H\big((0,1),t\big)=(0,1). \] is valid.

Fix any \(t \in [0,1]\), \(H\big((x_n,y_n),t\big) \to H\big((0,1),t\big)=(0,1)\) as \(x_n=\frac{1}{n} \to 0,y_n=1 \to 1\). Now fix \(n\), since \(H\big((x_n,y_n),t\big)\xrightarrow{t \to 1}(0,1)\). There is a moment \(t_n\) where \(H\big((x_n,y_n),t_n\big)=(\frac{1}{n},0)\). Since \(\{t_n\} \subset [0,1]\) where \([0,1]\) is compact, w.l.o.g. we can assume that \(t_n \to t_*\). Therefore, \(H\big((x_n,y_n),t_n\big) \to H\big((0,1),t_*\big)=(0,0)\). However, as the homotopy keeps \((0,1)\) fixed, \(H\big((0,1),t_*\big)=(0,1)\neq(0,0)\), a contradiction.

Clearly any convex set in Euclidean space is contractible, like \(E^{n+1}\). However, \(S^n, n\geq 0\) are not contractible.

Proposition 5

A space is contractible implies that the space is path-connected:

Proof. By corollary 4, for any point \(p\in X\), we can ‘homotoping’ the identity map \(\operatorname{id}_X\) to \(p\). Then for any point \(x,p \in X\), as \(\operatorname{id}_X \simeq p\), \(\gamma(t):=H(x,t)\) is a continuous path connecting \(x,p\).

By proposition 5, it follows that the pseudo-circle is not contractible since it is not path-connected.
