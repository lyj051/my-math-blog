Exercise 1

Prove that a bilinear form \(\varphi: \mathbb{R}^3 \times \mathbb{R}^3 \to \mathbb{R}\) is alternate if and only if \(\varphi(v, v) = 0\), for all \(v \in \mathbb{R}^3\).

Proof. \[ \varphi(v,v)+\varphi(v,w)+\varphi(w,w)+\varphi(w,v)=\varphi(v+w,v+w)=0 \]

Exercise 2

Prove that if \(i_1 < i_2 < \ldots < i_k\) and \(j_1 < j_2 < \ldots < j_k\), then
\[ (dx_{i_1} \wedge \ldots \wedge dx_{i_k})(e_{j_1}, \ldots, e_{j_k}) = \begin{cases} 1, & \text{if } i_1 = j_1, \ldots, i_k = j_k, \\ 0, & \text{otherwise.} \end{cases} \]

Proof. \[ \begin{aligned} & (dx_{i_1} \wedge \ldots \wedge dx_{i_k})(e_{j_1}, \ldots, e_{j_k}) \\ =&\operatorname{det}(dx_{i_s}(e_{j_t}))_{1\leq s,t \leq k} \end{aligned} \] If there exists some \(j_t\) is equal to none of \(i_s\), then the matrix has a zero column, \(\operatorname{det}=0\); Otherwise, \(i_1 = j_1, \ldots, i_k = j_k\) holds.

Exercise 3

Let \(\varphi\) be an exterior \(k\)-form, where \(k\) is an odd integer. Show that \(\varphi \wedge \varphi = 0\).

Proof. \[ \begin{aligned} \varphi \wedge\varphi=(-1)^{k^2}(\varphi \wedge \varphi) \end{aligned} \]

Exercise 4

Let \(\varphi, \psi\) and \(\theta\) the following forms in \(\mathbb{R}^3\):

\[ \varphi = xdx - ydy, \] \[ \psi = zdx \wedge dy + xdy \wedge dz, \] \[ \theta = zdy. \]

Compute: \(\varphi \wedge \psi\), \(\theta \wedge \varphi \wedge \psi\), \(d\varphi\), \(d\psi\), \(d\theta\).

Proof. \[ \begin{aligned} &(xdx - ydy)\wedge(zdx \wedge dy + xdy \wedge dz)=x^2dx\wedge dy \wedge dz \\ &\theta \wedge \varphi \wedge \psi=0 \\ &d\varphi=0 \\ &d\theta=dz \wedge dy \end{aligned} \]

Exercise 5

Let \(\omega\) be the 2-form in \(\mathbb{R}^{2n}\) given by
\[ \omega = dx_1 \wedge dx_2 + dx_3 \wedge dx_4 + \ldots + dx_{2n-1} \wedge dx_{2n}. \]

Compute the exterior product of \(n\) copies of \(\omega\).

Proof. claim: for any positive integer \(n\), \(dx_I=dx_1 \wedge \ldots\wedge dx_{2n}\) where \(dx_I\) is a form like \(\wedge_{k=1}^n(dx_{i_{2k-1}}\wedge dx_{i_{2k}})\); the proof is staightforward using induction on \(n\).

By claim, we know \[ \wedge^n \omega=\sum_{I}dx_I=n!dx_1 \wedge \ldots\wedge dx_{2n} \]

Exercise 6

Let \(f\colon \mathbf{R}^{n}\to \mathbf{R}^{n}\) be a differentiable map given by

\[ f(x_{1},\ldots ,x_{n}) = (y_{1},\ldots ,y_{n}), \]

and let \(\omega = d y_{1}\wedge \ldots \wedge d y_{n}\). Show that

\[ f^{*}\omega = \det(df) \, d x_{1}\wedge \ldots \wedge d x_{n}. \]

Proof. \[ \begin{aligned} f^*\omega&=df_1 \wedge \ldots \wedge df_n \\ &=\wedge_{i=1}^{n}(\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{\partial f_i}{\partial x_j}dx_j) \\ &=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j_k \text{ is a permutation}}\operatorname{sgn}((j_k)) \prod_{j=1}^{n}\frac{\partial f_i}{\partial x_{j_k}} dx_I\\ &=\det(df) \, d x_{1}\wedge \ldots \wedge d x_{n} \end{aligned} \]

Exercise 9: Divergence

A differentiable vector field \(\mathbf{v}\) in \(\mathbf{R}^{n}\) may be considered as a differentiable map \(v:\mathbf{R}^{n}\to \mathbf{R}^{n}\). We will define a function \(\operatorname{div} v:\mathbf{R}^{n}\to \mathbf{R}\) (to be called the divergence of \(v\)) as follows:

\[ (\operatorname{div}v)(p) = \operatorname{trace}(dv)_{p},\quad p\in \mathbf{R}^{n}, \]

where \((dv)_{p}:\mathbf{R}_{p}^{n}\to \mathbf{R}_{p}^{n}\) is the differential of \(\mathbf{v}\) at \(p\). Show that:

  1. If \(v = \sum_{i=1}^{n} a_{i} e_{i}\), where \(\{e_{i}\}\) is the canonical basis of \(\mathbf{R}^{n}\), then

\[ \operatorname{div}v = \sum \frac{\partial a_{i}}{\partial x_{i}}. \]

  1. If \(\omega\) denotes the differential 1-form obtained from \(\mathbf{v}\) by the canonical isomorphism induced by the inner product \(\langle , \rangle\) and \(\nu\) is the volume element of \(\mathbf{R}^{n}\) (Exercise 9), the divergence can be obtained as follows: \[ v \longrightarrow \omega \longrightarrow \star \omega \longrightarrow d(\star \omega) = (\operatorname{div}v)\nu, \]

Proof.

  1. \[ (\operatorname{div}v)(p)=\operatorname{trace}(dv)_{p}=\sum_{i=1}^n \frac{\partial a_{i}}{\partial x_{i}} \vert_{p} \] since \(v=\sum_{i=1}^n a_ie_i\) where \(a_i:\mathbf{R}^n \rightarrow \mathbf{R}\) is differentiable.

  2. Notice that \(\omega=\langle v,- \rangle=\sum a_i\langle e_i, - \rangle=\sum a_i dx_i\); it follows that \[ \begin{aligned} &^{\star}(\sum a_i dx_i)=\sum a_i (^{\star}dx_i)\\ &d(a_i) \wedge (^{\star}dx_i)=\sum \frac{\partial a_i}{\partial x_i}dx_1\wedge \ldots \wedge dx_n \\ &(\sum \frac{\partial a_i}{\partial x_i})dx_1\wedge \ldots \wedge dx_n = \operatorname{trace}(dv)\nu=(\operatorname{div}v)\nu \end{aligned} \]

Exercise 10: Gradiant

Given a differentiable function \(f:\mathbf{R}^{n}\to \mathbf{R}\), define a vector field \(\operatorname{grad} f\) in \(\mathbf{R}^{n}\) (the gradient of \(f\)) by

\[ \langle \operatorname{grad} f(p),u\rangle = (df)_{p}(u),\quad \text{for all } p\in \mathbf{R}^{n} \text{ and all } u\in \mathbf{R}_{p}^{n}. \]

Notice that \(\operatorname{grad} f\) is the vector field corresponding to the 1-form \(df\) in the canonical isomorphism. Show that:

  1. In the canonical basis \(\{e_{i}\}\) of \(\mathbf{R}^{n}\),

\[ \operatorname{grad} f = \sum \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{i}} e_{i}. \]

  1. If \(p\in \mathbf{R}^{n}\) is such that \(\operatorname{grad} f(p)\neq 0\), then \(\operatorname{grad} f(p)\) is perpendicular to the “level surface” \(\{q\in \mathbf{R}^{n}; f(q) = f(p)\}\).

  2. The linear map \((df)_{p}:\mathbf{R}_{p}^{n}\to \mathbf{R}\) restricted to the unit sphere with center at \(p\) reaches its maximum for \(v = \operatorname{grad} f / |\operatorname{grad} f|\).

Proof. A vector field \(\mathbf{v}\) is uniquely determined by the linear forms \(\langle \mathbf{v}(p),- \rangle \in (\mathbf{R}_p^{n})^*\).

  1. \[ \begin{aligned} &(\sum_{i=1}^n \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{i}} e_{i})(p)=\sum_{i=1}^n \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{i}}\vert_p e_{i} \\ \Rightarrow &[(\sum_{i=1}^n \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{i}} e_{i})(p)](u)=\sum_{i=1}^n \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{i}}\vert_p u_{i} \\ &=(df)_p(u) \end{aligned} \] Hence, \(\operatorname{grad} f = \sum_{i=1}^n \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{i}} e_{i}\).

  2. Let \(\gamma: (-\varepsilon, \varepsilon) \to S\) be any smooth curve with \(\gamma(0) = p\). Since \(\gamma(t) \in S\) for all \(t\),

\[ f(\gamma(t)) = f(p), \quad \forall t \in (-\varepsilon, \varepsilon). \]

Differentiating with respect to \(t\) and applying the chain rule gives

\[ (df)_{\gamma(t)}(\gamma'(t)) = 0. \]

At \(t=0\),

\[ (df)_p(\gamma'(0)) = 0. \]

By the definition of the gradient, \(\langle \operatorname{grad} f(p), u \rangle = (df)_p(u)\) for all \(u \in \mathbf{R}_p^n\). Taking \(u = \gamma'(0)\),

\[ \langle \operatorname{grad} f(p), \gamma'(0) \rangle = 0. \]

Thus \(\operatorname{grad} f(p) \perp \gamma'(0)\). Since \(\gamma'(0)\) is an arbitrary tangent vector to \(S\) at \(p\), the gradient is orthogonal to every tangent direction, hence perpendicular to the level surface \(S\).

  1. By 1, we have \[ \Vert (df)_p(u) \Vert= \Vert \operatorname{grad}f(p) \cdot u \Vert \leq \Vert \operatorname{grad}f(p) \Vert \cdot \Vert u \Vert \] and the equality holds when \(u\parallel \operatorname{grad}f(p)\); thus \(u=\operatorname{grad}f(p)/\vert \operatorname{grad}f(p)\vert\) where \((df)_p\) attains its maximum on the unit sphere.
Exercise 11: Laplacian

Given a differentiable function \(f:\mathbf{R}^{n}\to \mathbf{R}\), we will define the Laplacian \(\Delta f:\mathbf{R}^{n}\to \mathbf{R}\) by

\[ \Delta f = \operatorname{div}(\operatorname{grad} f). \]

Show that:

  1. \[ \Delta f = \sum \frac{\partial^{2} f}{\partial x_{i}^{2}}, \]

  2. \[ \Delta (fg) = f\Delta g + g\Delta f + 2\langle \operatorname{grad} f, \operatorname{grad} g \rangle, \]

  3. \(d * (df) = (\Delta f)\nu\), where \(\nu\) is the volume element of \(\mathbf{R}^{n}\).

Proof.

  1. \[ \Delta f = \operatorname{div}(\operatorname{grad} f)=\operatorname{div}(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\cfrac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}e_i)=\sum_{i=1}^n\frac{\partial^2f}{\partial x_i^2} \]

  2. \[ \begin{aligned} \Delta (fg)&=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\partial^2(fg)}{\partial x_i^2}=\sum_{i=1}^n \frac{\partial}{\partial x_i}(f\frac{\partial g}{\partial x_i}+g\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}) \\ &=\sum_{i=1}^nf\frac{\partial^2 g}{\partial x_i^2}+g\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x_i^2}+2\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}\frac{\partial g}{\partial x_i} \\ &=f \Delta g +g \Delta f+2 \langle \operatorname{grad}f,\operatorname{grad} g \rangle \end{aligned} \]

  3. \[ d (^{\star} df)=d(^{\star}(\sum_{i=1}^n\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}dx_i))=(\sum_{i=1}^n \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x_i^2})\nu \]

Exercise 14: Poincaré’s lemma for 1-forms

Let

\[ \omega = a(x,y,z)\,dx + b(x,y,z)\,dy + c(x,y,z)\,dz \]

be a differentiable 1-form in \(\mathbf{R}^{3}\) such that \(d\omega = 0\). Define \(f\colon \mathbf{R}^{3}\to \mathbf{R}\) by

\[ f(x,y,z) = \int_{0}^{1} \big( a(tx,ty,tz)x + b(tx,ty,tz)y + c(tx,ty,tz)z \big) \, dt. \]

Show that \(df = \omega\). (This means that the condition \(d\omega = 0\), which is satisfied when \(\omega = df\), is in \(\mathbf{R}^{3}\) also sufficient for the existence of an \(f\) such that \(\omega = df\). We have used \(\mathbf{R}^{3}\) for notational convenience but the result holds for \(\mathbf{R}^{n}\). Also, if \(\omega\) is only defined in an open set \(U\subset \mathbf{R}^{n}\), the result still holds in a neighborhood of each \(p\in U\).)

Tip

Notice that \(d\omega = 0\) implies that

\[ \frac{\partial b}{\partial x} = \frac{\partial a}{\partial y},\quad \frac{\partial c}{\partial x} = \frac{\partial a}{\partial z},\quad \frac{\partial b}{\partial z} = \frac{\partial c}{\partial y}, \]

and use the identity

\[ a(x,y,z) = \int_{0}^{1} \frac{d}{dt} \big( a(tx,ty,tz)t \big) \, dt = \int_{0}^{1} a(tx,ty,tz) \, dt + \int_{0}^{1} t (a_{1}x + a_{2}y + a_{3}z) \, dt, \]

where \(a_{1}, a_{2}\) and \(a_{3}\) denote the partial derivatives of \(a(tx, ty, tz)\) relative to the first, second and third argument, respectively.

Proof. We wish to show that \(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}=a(x,y,z),\dots,\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}=c(x,y,z)\).

  • \[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial f(x,y,z)}{\partial x} &= \int_{0}^{1} \frac{\partial}{\partial x}\big( a(tx,ty,tz)x + b(tx,ty,tz)y + c(tx,ty,tz)z \big) \, dt \\ &=\int_0^1 a(tx,ty,tz)\,dt+\int_0^1 t\,(xa_1+yb_1+zc_1)\,dt \\ &=\int_0^1 a(tx,ty,tz)\,dt+\int_0^1 t\,(xa_1+ya_2+za_3)\,dt \end{aligned} \]

  • \[ \frac{d}{dt}\big(t \cdot a(tx,ty,tz)\big)=a(tx,ty,tz)+t\big(xa_1+ya_2+za_3\big) \] Integrating both sides wrt \(t\) from \(0\) to \(1\): \[ \int_0^1 \frac{d}{dt}\big(t\cdot a(tx,ty,tz)\big)\,dt=\int_0^1 a(tx,ty,tz)\,dt+\int_0^1t(xa_1+ya_2+za_3)\,dt \] The LHS equals to \[ [t\cdot a(tx,ty,tz)]_0^1=a(x,y,z) \]

Hence we have \(\partial f/\partial x=a(x,y,z)\) etc.

Exercise 15

We say that a differentiable vector field \(v\) defined in an open set \(U \subset \mathbf{R}^{n}\) derives locally from a potential if for each \(p \in U\) there exists a neighborhood \(V \ni p, V \subset U\), and a differentiable function \(g: V \to \mathbf{R}\) (to be called the potential) such that \(v = \operatorname{grad} g\).

  1. Show that \(v\) derives locally from a potential if and only if \(\operatorname{rot} v = 0\).

Show that \(v\) derives locally from a potential \(g\):

and that \(\Delta g = 0\).

Proof.

If \(v\) derives locally from a potential, \(v\) admits a local representation \[ v=\operatorname{grad} f=\sum\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}e_i \] then \[ \begin{aligned} \omega&=\sum\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}dx_i=df \\ d\omega&=d^2f=0 \qquad \text{ since } f \text{ is a }0\text{-form locally} \end{aligned} \] Converely, if \(d\omega=0\), by Poincaré’s lemma for 1-forms, \(\omega=df\) and thus \(v=\operatorname{grad}f\).

A function \(g: \mathbf{R}^{3} \to \mathbf{R}\) is said to be homogeneous of degree \(k\) if \(g(tx, ty, tz) = t^{k} g(x, y, z)\), for \(t > 0\), \((x, y, z) \in \mathbf{R}^{3}\). Prove that:

  1. If \(g\) is differentiable and homogeneous of degree \(k\), then (Euler’s relation)

\[ x g_{x} + y g_{y} + z g_{z} = k g. \]

Tip

Differentiate \(g(tx, ty, tz) = t^{k} g(x, y, z)\) in \(t\) and set \(t = 1\).

  1. If the differential form

\[ \omega = a\, dx + b\, dy + c\, dz \]

is such that \(a, b\) and \(c\) are homogeneous of degree \(k\) and \(d\omega = 0\), then \(\omega = df\), where \[ f = \frac{x a + y b + z c}{k + 1}. \]

Tip 3

Notice that \(d\omega = 0\) implies that

\[ \frac{\partial b}{\partial x} = \frac{\partial a}{\partial y},\quad \frac{\partial c}{\partial x} = \frac{\partial a}{\partial z},\quad \frac{\partial b}{\partial z} = \frac{\partial c}{\partial y}, \]

and apply Euler’s relation.

  1. If the differential form

\[ \sigma = a\, dy\wedge dz + b\, dz\wedge dx + c\, dx\wedge dy \]

is such that \(a, b\) and \(c\) are homogeneous of degree \(k\) and \(d\sigma = 0\), then \(\sigma = d\gamma\), where

\[ \gamma = \frac{(z b - y c)\, dx + (x c - z a)\, dy + (y a - x b)\, dz}{k + 2}. \]
