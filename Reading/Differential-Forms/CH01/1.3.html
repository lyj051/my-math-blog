Exercise 1: A geometric definition of the \(\star\) operation

An element \(\phi \in \Lambda^{k}(\mathbf{R}_{p}^{n})^{*}\) is called decomposable if \(\phi = \phi_{1}\wedge \ldots \wedge \phi_{k}\), where \(\phi_{i}\), \(i = 1,\ldots ,k\), are linearly independent elements of \(\Lambda^{1}(\mathbf{R}_{p}^{n})^{*}\simeq (\mathbf{R}_{p}^{n})^{*}\). Prove that:

  1. If \(\phi_{i} = \sum_{j} a_{i j}\beta_{j}\), \(i = 1,\ldots ,k\), \(\beta_{j}\in (\mathbf{R}_{p}^{n})^{*}\), and \(\det(a_{i j}) = 1\), then \(\phi_{1}\wedge \ldots \wedge \phi_{k} = \beta_{1}\wedge \ldots \wedge \beta_{k}\); thus, a decomposable element may have more than one representation.

  2. If \(\phi_{1}\wedge \ldots \wedge \phi_{k} = \beta_{1}\wedge \ldots \wedge \beta_{k} = \phi\) are two representations of \(\phi\), then \(\phi_{i} = \sum_{j} a_{i j}\beta_{j}\), with \(\det(a_{i j}) = 1\).

Tip 1

Hint: Extend the \(\beta_{j}\) into a basis \(\beta_{1},\ldots ,\beta_{k},\beta_{k + 1},\ldots \beta_{n}\) of \((\mathbf{R}_{p}^{n})^{*}\) and write \[ \phi_{i} = \sum_{j} a_{i j}\beta_{j} + \sum_{\eta} b_{i\eta}\beta_{\eta},\qquad \eta = k + 1,\ldots ,n. \]

Notice that \(\beta_{1}\wedge \dots \wedge \beta_{k}\wedge \phi_{i} = \phi_{1}\wedge \dots \wedge\phi_{k}\wedge \phi_{i} = 0\). This implies that \[ \sum_{\eta} b_{i\eta}\beta_{1}\wedge \ldots \wedge \beta_{k}\wedge \beta_{\eta} = 0, \]

and since \(\beta_{1}\wedge \ldots \wedge \beta_{k}\wedge \beta_{\eta}\) are linearly independent, \(b_{i\eta} = 0\).

  1. If \(\phi = \phi_{1}\wedge \ldots \wedge \phi_{k}\) is decomposable, the vectors \(v_{1},\ldots ,v_{k}\in \mathbf{R}_{p}^{n}\), where \(v_{i}\leftrightarrow \phi_{i}\) is the correspondence induced by the inner product \(\langle , \rangle\) of \(\mathbf{R}^{n}\), are linearly independent, and the subspace of \(\mathbf{R}^{n}\) generated by them does not depend on the representation of \(\phi\). This will be called the subspace of \(\phi\).

  2. If \(\phi = \phi_{1}\wedge \ldots \wedge \phi_{k}\) is decomposable, the \(k\)-volume of the solid generated by \(v_{1},\ldots ,v_{k}\) does not depend on the representation of \(\phi\). This will be called the volume of \(\phi\).

5.If \(\phi = \phi_{1}\wedge \ldots \wedge \phi_{k}\) is decomposable, define \(\ast \phi\) as an element of \(\Lambda^{n - k}(\mathbf{R}_{p}^{n})^{*}\) with the following properties:

  • the subspace of \(\ast \phi\) is perpendicular to the subspace of \(\phi\).

  • the volume of \(\ast \phi\) is equal to the volume of \(\phi\).

  • \(\phi \wedge \ast \phi\) is positive, i.e., its value in a positive basis of \(\mathbf{R}_{p}^{n}\) is positive.

    Prove that \(\ast \phi\) is well defined.

Tip 2

Hint: Let \(v_{i}\leftrightarrow \phi_{i}\), \(i = 1,\ldots ,k\), as in (c) and let \(W\) be the subspace of \(\mathbf{R}_{p}^{n}\) generated by the \(v_{i}\)’s. In \(W^{\perp}\) consider an orthonormal basis \(e_{k + 1},\ldots ,e_{n}\) so that the basis \(v_{1},\ldots ,v_{k},e_{k + 1},\ldots ,e_{n}\) of \(\mathbf{R}_{p}^{n}\) is positive. Define \(\phi_{j}\in (\mathbf{R}_{p}^{n})^{*}, j = k + 1,\ldots ,n\), by the correspondence \(\phi_{j}\leftrightarrow e_{j}\) mentioned in (c), and let \(\lambda >0\) be the volume of \(W\). Check that \(\lambda \, \phi_{k + 1}\wedge \ldots \wedge \phi_{n}\) satisfies 1, 2 and 3.

  1. Let \(v_{1},v_{2}\) be two vectors in \(\mathbf{R}^{3}\) and let \(\phi_{1}\leftrightarrow v_{1},\phi_{2}\leftrightarrow v_{2}\) be the one-forms given by the correspondence in (c). Define the vector product \(v_{1}\times v_{2}\leftrightarrow \ast (\phi_{1}\wedge \phi_{2})\) and describe geometrically the vector \(v_{1}\times v_{2}\).

  2. A \(k\)-form \(\omega\) in \(\mathbf{R}^{n}\) is decomposable if \(\omega(p)\) is decomposable for each \(p\in \mathbf{R}^{n}\). Every \(k\)-form in \(\mathbf{R}^{n}\) is a linear combination of decomposable \(k\)-forms of the type \(d x_{i_{1}}\wedge \ldots \wedge d x_{i_{k}}\). Show that with the above definition, \(\ast (d x_{i_{1}}\wedge \ldots \wedge d x_{i_{k}})\) gives the same expression as in Exercise 10.

Proof.

  1. \[ \varphi_1 \wedge \ldots \wedge \varphi_k=(\sum a_{1j}\beta_j)\wedge\ldots\wedge(\sum a_{nj}\beta_j)=\operatorname{det}(a_{ij})\beta_1\wedge\ldots\wedge\beta_k \]

  2. It’s clear by the tip. Indeed, representations of a decomposable element have the same orientations.

    • As \(\langle v_i,- \rangle=\varphi_i\), the linear independence of \(v_i\) follows from the linear independence of \(\varphi_i\).
    • Suppose that \(\beta_1\wedge\ldots\wedge\beta_k\) is another representation of \(\varphi\) and \(u_1,\ldots,u_k\) are the corresponded vectors. As \(\beta_i\) is a linear combination of \(v_i\)’s, so is each \(u_i\). Then the subspace spanned by the \(u_i\)’s are contained in that spanned by \(v_i\)’s. Conversely, the subspace spanned by the \(v_i\)’s are contained in that spanned by \(u_i\)’s. Thus the subspace of a decomposible \(k\)-form \(\varphi\) does not depend on its representation.

  4. By the definition of volume, set the \(k\)-volume element \(\nu(e_1,\ldots,e_k)\) where \((e_i)\)’s is the canonical basis of \(\operatorname{Subspace}(\varphi)\); It follows that \[ \operatorname{vol}(v_1,\ldots,v_k)=\nu(v_1,\ldots,v_k)=\nu(\sum a_{1j}u_1,\ldots,a_{kj}b_k)=\det(a_{ij})\nu(u_1,\ldots,u_k)=\nu(u_1,\ldots,u_k) \]

  5. Let \(v_1, \ldots, v_k\) span \(W=\operatorname{Subsapce}(\varphi)\) and set \(\lambda=\operatorname{vol}(v_1,\ldots,v_k)\)

    • Choose an orthonormal basis \(e_{k+1},\ldots,e_n\) of \(W^{\perp}\) such that \(v_1,\ldots,v_k,e_{k+1},\ldots,e_n\) forms a positively oriented basis of \(\mathbf{R}_p^n\) (i.e. Set the \(n\)-linear alternating mapping \(\Delta=(dx_1)_p \wedge \ldots \wedge (dx_n)_p\); \(\Delta(v_1,\ldots,v_k,e_{k+1},\ldots,e_n)>0\)).
    • Let \(\varphi_j \leftrightarrow e_j\) for \(j=k+1, \dots, n\).
    • Define the candidate form \(^\star(\varphi)=\lambda \, \varphi_{k+1}\wedge \ldots \wedge \varphi_n\). It’s clear that this definition satisfies the three conditions.

    well definition:

    We choose another representation \(\beta_1\wedge\dots\wedge \beta_k\) of \(\varphi\), and set the corresponded bases of \(W\): \(u_1,\dots,u_k\);let \(f_{k+1},\dots,f_n\) be a basis of \(W^\perp\) such that they form a positively oriented basis of \(\mathbf{R}^n_p\).

    • \(\operatorname{vol}(u_1,\ldots,u_k)=\lambda\) is clear.
    • As \(f_{k+1},\dots,f_n\) and \(e_{k+1},\dots,e_n\) have the same orientation and are bases of \(W^\perp\), it follows that \[ f_i=\sum_{j=k+1}^{n} q_{ij}e_j, \qquad i=k+1,\dots,n \] where \(Q=[q_{ij}] \in \operatorname{SO}(n-k)\); hence \[ \beta_{k+1}\wedge\dots\wedge \beta_n=\det(Q)(\varphi_{k+1}\wedge\dots\wedge \varphi_n) \]
    • thus \(\varphi_{k+1}\wedge \ldots \wedge \varphi_n=\beta_{k+1}\wedge\dots\wedge \beta_{n}\)

    Therefore \(^\star(\varphi)\) is well-defined.

  6. A vector \(v\) in \(\mathbf{R}_p^3\) is defined to be the vector product \(v_1 \times v_2\) if \(\langle v,v_i\rangle=0\), \(v_1,v_2,v\) is a positively oriented basis and \(\Vert v \Vert=\operatorname{vol}(v_1,v_2)\).

  7. By the geometric definition, (suppose that \(i_1<\dots<i_k\) and \(e_1,\dots,e_n\) is a positive orientation.) \[ ^\star(dx_{i_1}\wedge\ldots\wedge dx_{i_k})=\operatorname{vol}(e_{i_1},\dots,e_{i_k}) \,\varphi_{j_1}\wedge\ldots\wedge\varphi_{j_{n-k}} \] where \(\varphi_{j_t} \leftrightarrow e^\prime_{j_t}\) and \(e^\prime_{j_t},\ t=1,\dots,n-k\) are a rearrangement of \(e_{j_t}\) which are the complement of \(e_{i_t}\) and in ascending order; thus \[ \varphi_{j_1}\wedge\ldots\wedge\varphi_{j_{n-k}}=(-1)^{\sigma}e_{j_1}\wedge\dots\wedge e_{j_{n-k}} \] where \(\sigma\) is the sign of \((1,\dots,n)\to (i_1,\dots,i_k,j_1,\dots,j_{n-k})\)

Exercise 2: Poincaré’s lemma for 1-forms

Let

\[ \omega = a(x,y,z)\,dx + b(x,y,z)\,dy + c(x,y,z)\,dz \]

be a differentiable 1-form in \(\mathbf{R}^{3}\) such that \(d\omega = 0\). Define \(f\colon \mathbf{R}^{3}\to \mathbf{R}\) by

\[ f(x,y,z) = \int_{0}^{1} \big( a(tx,ty,tz)x + b(tx,ty,tz)y + c(tx,ty,tz)z \big) \, dt. \]

Show that \(df = \omega\). (This means that the condition \(d\omega = 0\), which is satisfied when \(\omega = df\), is in \(\mathbf{R}^{3}\) also sufficient for the existence of an \(f\) such that \(\omega = df\). We have used \(\mathbf{R}^{3}\) for notational convenience but the result holds for \(\mathbf{R}^{n}\). Also, if \(\omega\) is only defined in an open set \(U\subset \mathbf{R}^{n}\), the result still holds in a neighborhood of each \(p\in U\).)

Tip

Notice that \(d\omega = 0\) implies that

\[ \frac{\partial b}{\partial x} = \frac{\partial a}{\partial y},\quad \frac{\partial c}{\partial x} = \frac{\partial a}{\partial z},\quad \frac{\partial b}{\partial z} = \frac{\partial c}{\partial y}, \]

and use the identity

\[ a(x,y,z) = \int_{0}^{1} \frac{d}{dt} \big( a(tx,ty,tz)t \big) \, dt = \int_{0}^{1} a(tx,ty,tz) \, dt + \int_{0}^{1} t (a_{1}x + a_{2}y + a_{3}z) \, dt, \]

where \(a_{1}, a_{2}\) and \(a_{3}\) denote the partial derivatives of \(a(tx, ty, tz)\) relative to the first, second and third argument, respectively.

Proof. We wish to show that \(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}=a(x,y,z),\dots,\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}=c(x,y,z)\).

  • \[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial f(x,y,z)}{\partial x} &= \int_{0}^{1} \frac{\partial}{\partial x}\big( a(tx,ty,tz)x + b(tx,ty,tz)y + c(tx,ty,tz)z \big) \, dt \\ &=\int_0^1 a(tx,ty,tz)\,dt+\int_0^1 t\,(xa_1+yb_1+zc_1)\,dt \\ &=\int_0^1 a(tx,ty,tz)\,dt+\int_0^1 t\,(xa_1+ya_2+za_3)\,dt \end{aligned} \]

  • \[ \frac{d}{dt}\big(t \cdot a(tx,ty,tz)\big)=a(tx,ty,tz)+t\big(xa_1+ya_2+za_3\big) \] Integrating both sides wrt \(t\) from \(0\) to \(1\): \[ \int_0^1 \frac{d}{dt}\big(t\cdot a(tx,ty,tz)\big)\,dt=\int_0^1 a(tx,ty,tz)\,dt+\int_0^1t(xa_1+ya_2+za_3)\,dt \] The LHS equals to \[ [t\cdot a(tx,ty,tz)]_0^1=a(x,y,z) \]

Hence we have \(\partial f/\partial x=a(x,y,z)\) etc.

Exercise 3

We say that a differentiable vector field \(v\) defined in an open set \(U \subset \mathbf{R}^{n}\) derives locally from a potential if for each \(p \in U\) there exists a neighborhood \(V \ni p, V \subset U\), and a differentiable function \(g: V \to \mathbf{R}\) (to be called the potential) such that \(v = \operatorname{grad} g\).

  1. Let \(v\) be as above and let \(\omega\) be the 1-form corresponding to \(v\), i.e., \(\omega(u) = \langle v, u \rangle\), for all \(u \in \mathbf{R}^{n}\). Show that \(v\) derives locally from a potential if and only if \(d\omega = 0\).

Hint: Use the local form of Poincaré’s lemma for 1-forms.

  1. Show that \(v\) derives locally from a potential if and only if \(\operatorname{rot} v = 0\).

  2. Let \(v\) be the vector field (electric attraction):

\[ v(p) = -\frac{1}{(x^{2} + y^{2} + z^{2})^{3/2}} (x, y, z), \qquad p \in \mathbf{R}^{3} \setminus \{(0,0,0)\}. \]

Show that \(v\) derives locally from a potential \(g\):

\[ g = \frac{1}{(x^{2} + y^{2} + z^{2})^{1/2}} + \text{const}, \]

and that \(\Delta g = 0\).

Proof.

  1. If \(v\) derives locally from a potential, \(v\) admits a local representation \[ v=\operatorname{grad} f=\sum\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}e_i \] then \[ \begin{aligned} \omega&=\sum\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}dx_i=df \\ d\omega&=d^2f=0 \qquad \text{ since } f \text{ is a }0\text{-form locally} \end{aligned} \] Converely, if \(d\omega=0\), by Poincaré’s lemma for 1-forms, \(\omega=df\) and thus \(v=\operatorname{grad}f\).

  2. \[ \operatorname{rot}(v)=0 \iff d\omega=0 \iff v \text{ derives locally from a potential} \]

  3. Straightforward.

Exercise 4

A function \(g: \mathbf{R}^{3} \to \mathbf{R}\) is said to be homogeneous of degree \(k\) if \(g(tx, ty, tz) = t^{k} g(x, y, z)\), for \(t > 0\), \((x, y, z) \in \mathbf{R}^{3}\). Prove that:

  1. If \(g\) is differentiable and homogeneous of degree \(k\), then (Euler’s relation)

\[ x g_{x} + y g_{y} + z g_{z} = k g. \]

Tip

Differentiate \(g(tx, ty, tz) = t^{k} g(x, y, z)\) in \(t\) and set \(t = 1\).

  1. If the differential form

\[ \omega = a\, dx + b\, dy + c\, dz \]

is such that \(a, b\) and \(c\) are homogeneous of degree \(k\) and \(d\omega = 0\), then \(\omega = df\), where \[ f = \frac{x a + y b + z c}{k + 1}. \]

Tip 3

Notice that \(d\omega = 0\) implies that

\[ \frac{\partial b}{\partial x} = \frac{\partial a}{\partial y},\quad \frac{\partial c}{\partial x} = \frac{\partial a}{\partial z},\quad \frac{\partial b}{\partial z} = \frac{\partial c}{\partial y}, \]

and apply Euler’s relation.

  1. If the differential form

\[ \sigma = a\, dy\wedge dz + b\, dz\wedge dx + c\, dx\wedge dy \]

is such that \(a, b\) and \(c\) are homogeneous of degree \(k\) and \(d\sigma = 0\), then \(\sigma = d\gamma\), where

\[ \gamma = \frac{(z b - y c)\, dx + (x c - z a)\, dy + (y a - x b)\, dz}{k + 2}. \]

Proof.

  1. Differentiate \(g(tx, ty, tz) = t^{k} g(x, y, z)\) in \(t\) and we get: \[ x\,g_1(tx,ty,tz)+y\,g_2(tx,ty,tz)+z\,g_3(tx,ty,tz)=k\,t^{k-1}\,g(x,y,z) \] Then let \(t=1\) and we have proved the Euler relation.

  2. It’s clear by the tip

    • \[ a_x+b_y+c_z=0 \]

\[ \begin{aligned} d(zb-yc)\wedge dx&=\big((zb_x-yc_x)dx+(zb_y-c-yc_y)d_y+(b+zb_z-yc_z)dz\big)\wedge dx \\ &=(c+yc_y-zb_y)dx\wedge dy+(yc_z-b-zb_z)dx\wedge dz \\ d(xc-za)\wedge dy&=(c+xc_x-za_x)dx\wedge dy +(za_z+a-xc_z)dy\wedge dz \\ d(ya-zb)\wedge dz&=(a+ya_y-xb_y)dy\wedge dz+(ya_x-b-xb_x)dx \wedge dz d(zb-yc)\wedge dx+d(xc-za)\wedge dy+d(ya-zb)\wedge dz&=(k+2)c \, dx\wedge dy+(k+2)b \,dx\wedge dz +(k+2)a \,dy\wedge dz \end{aligned} \]
