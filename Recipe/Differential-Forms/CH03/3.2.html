Differentiable Manifolds

Differential Forms and Applications
P.Do Carmo
Author

Eugene Lee

Published

September 3, 2026

Definition 1

An \(n\)-dimensional differentiable manifold is a set \(M\) together with a family of injective maps \(f_{\alpha}:U_{\alpha}\rightarrow M\) of open sets \(U_{\alpha}\subset \mathbf{R}^n\) into \(M\) such that:

  1. \(\bigcup_{\alpha}f_{\alpha}(U_{\alpha})=M\).

  2. For each pair \(\alpha,\beta\), with \(f_{\alpha}(U_\alpha)\cap f_\beta(U_\beta)=W\neq \emptyset\), the sets \(f_\alpha^{-1}(W)\) and \(f_\beta^{-1}(W)\) are open sets in \(\mathbf{R}^n\) and the maps \(f^{-1}_\beta\circ f_\alpha,f^{-1}_\alpha\circ f_\beta\) are differentiable.

  3. The family \(\{(U_\alpha,f_\alpha)\}\) is maximal relative to conditions 1,2.

A family \(\{(U_\alpha,f_\alpha)\}=\mathcal{A}\) satisfying the conditions 1 and 2 is called a differentiable structure (or atlas) on \(M\). A pair \((U_\alpha,f_\alpha)\in\mathcal{A}\) with \(p\in f_\alpha(U_\alpha)\) is called a parametrization (or coordinate system,or chart) of \(M\) at \(p\); \(f_\alpha(U_\alpha)\) is called a coordinate neighbourhood of \(p\).

Condition 3 is a technical condition. Actually we can always extend a differentiable structure into a maximal one, by adjoining to the given structure all parametrizations that together with some parametrization of the given structure satisfy condition 2.

Lemma 1

If \(\mathcal{A}=\{(U_\alpha,h_\alpha)\}\) is a differentiable structure on \(M\), then \(\mathcal{A}\) is contained in a unique maximal differentiable structure on \(M\).

Proof. Let \(\mathcal{A}^\prime\) be the following set; \[ \mathcal{A}^\prime=\{(V,g):\mathcal{A}\cup(V,g)\text{ is a differentiable structure}\} \] We check that \(\mathcal{A}^\prime\) is a differentiable structure on \(M\) and then it’s clearly the unique maxiaml atlas containing \(\mathcal{A}\).

Let \((U,f),(V,g)\) be any two charts in \(\mathcal{A}\) with \(f(U)\cap g(V)=W\neq \emptyset\). We wish to show that \(g^{-1}\circ f:f^{-1}(W)\rightarrow \mathbf{R}^n\) and \(f^{-1}\circ g\) are differentiable.

Clearly we have \(W=\bigcup_{\alpha}h_{\alpha}(U_\alpha)\cap W=\bigcup_{\alpha}W_\alpha\) and thus \(f^{-1}(W)=\bigcup_\alpha f^{-1}(W_\alpha)\); Since \(F_\alpha=f(U)\cap h_\alpha(U_\alpha)\supset W_\alpha\), \(f^{-1}(F_\alpha)\supset f^{-1}(W_\alpha)\); then \(h_{\alpha}^{-1}\circ f\) is differentiable on \(f^{-1}(W_\alpha)\); Since \(G_\alpha=g(V)\cap h_\alpha(U_\alpha)\supset W_\alpha\), \(g^{-1}\circ h_\alpha\) is differentiable on \(h_{\alpha}(W_\alpha)\). Thus \(g^{-1}\circ f=g^{-1}\circ h_{\alpha}\circ h_{\alpha}^{-1}\circ f\) is differentiable on \(f^{-1}(W)\); this completes the proof.

Remark.

  1. A differentiable structure on a set \(M\) induces in a natural way a topology in \(M\). It suffices to define that \(A\subset M\) is an open set if \(f_{\alpha}^{-1}(A\cap f_{\alpha}(U_{\alpha}))\) is an open set in \(\mathbf{R}^n\), for all \(\alpha\). It is easily checked that this defines a topology in \(M\) in which the sets \(f_{\alpha}(U_{\alpha})\) are open and the maps \(f_\alpha\) are continuos.

  2. The natural topology defined above can be very strange. In particular, it can happen that one (or both) of the following axioms do not hold:

    1. Axiom of Hausdorff: Give two distinct points of \(M\) there exist neighbourhoods of these points that do not intersect.

    2. Axiom of countable basis: \(M\) can be covered by a countable number of coordinate neighbourhoods (we say then that \(M\) has a countable basis)

    Axiom 1 is essential to prove that the limit of a convering sequence is unique, and axiom 2 is essential for the existence of a partition of unity.

  3. We shall assume, from now on, that all the manifolds to be considered are Hausdorff and have a countable basis.

Example 2

The simplest example of a differentiable structure consists of \(\mathbf{R}^n\) with an atlas \(\mathcal{A}\) of only one pair, \((\mathbf{R}^n,\operatorname{id})\). We can easily make the atlas bigger;We can adjoin \((U,f)\) if \(U\xrightarrow{f}V\) are homeomorphic with \(f,f^{-1}\) are differentiable. Indeed we can adjoin as many such \(f\)’s as we like (proof).

Less trivial examples are the following ones.

Example 3: The real projective plane \(P^2(\mathbf{R})\)

We will denote by \(P^2(\mathbf{R})\) the all lines in \(\mathbf{R}^3\) that pass through the origin \((0,0,0)\) of \(\mathbf{R}^3\), i.e., \(P^2(\mathbf{R})\) is the set of all ‘directions’ in \(\mathbf{R}^3\).

We want to introduce a differentiable structure in \(P^2(\mathbf{R})\). For that, let \((x,y,z)\in \mathbf{R}^3\), and notice that \(P^2(\mathbf{R})\) is the quotient space of \(\mathbf{R}^3-\{(0,0,0)\}\) by the equivalence relation \(\sim\): \[ (x,y,z)\sim(\lambda x,\lambda y,\lambda z),\qquad \lambda\in\mathbf{R},\quad \lambda\neq0 \] The points of \(P^2(\mathbf{R})\) will be denoted by \([x,y,z]\).

We now define sets \(V_1,V_2,V_3\) in \(P^2(\mathbf{R})\) by: \[ \begin{aligned} &V_1=\{[x,y,z]:x\neq0\}\\ &V_2=\{[x,y,z]:y\neq0\}\\ &V_3=\{[x,y,z]:z\neq0\} \end{aligned} \] and maps \(f_i:\mathbf{R}^2\rightarrow V_i,\,i=1,2,3\) by \[ f_1(u,v)=[1,u,v],\quad f_2(u,v)=[u,1,v],\quad f_3(u,v)=[u,v,1] \] where \((u,v)\in \mathbf{R}^2\).

Geometrically \(V_2\), for instance, is the set of all lines that pass through origin and do not belong to the plane \(xOz\). We claim that the family \(\{(\mathbf{R}^2,f_i)\}\) is a differentiable structure for \(P^2(\mathbf{R})\).

Actually we need only show that \(f_i^{-1}(V_i\cap V_i)\) is open in \(\mathbf{R}^2\) and \(f_j^{-1}\circ f_i\) is differentiable. Let us consider the case \(i=1,j=2\); the other cases are entirely analogous.

The points of \(f_1^{-1}(V_1\cap V_2)\) are of the form \((u,v)\) with \(u\neq0\); thus the set \(f_1^{-1}(V_1\cap V_2)\) is open in \(\mathbf{R}^2\) and \[ f_2^{-1}\circ f_1(u,v)=f_2^{-1}([1,u,v])=f_2^{-1}(1/u,1,v/u)=(1/u,v/u) \] is differentiable, as we claimed.

Example 4: The Klein bottle

The Klein bottle is the subset of \(\mathbf{R}^4\) defined as follows (its projection in \(\mathbf{R}^3\) is given in the video above); let \(Ox,Oy,Oz,Ow\) be the four coordinate axis in \(\mathbf{R}^4\). Let \(S\) be a circ with radius \(r\), contained in the plane \(xOz\), with center \(C\) in the axix \(Ox\) so that \(C\) is at a distance \(a>r\) from 0. The Klein bottle is generated by rotating this circ around \(Oz\) in such a way that when the center \(C\) has described a rotation of an angle \(u\) in the plane \(xOz\), the plane of \(S\) has described a rotation of an angle \(u\) around \(Oc\) in the \(3\)-space \(OCOzOw\) (this is possible because we are in \(\mathbf{R}^4\) but the projection of the moving circle in \(\mathbf{R}^3\) is twisted into a line where the intersection happens when \(u=\pi\)).

Let \(u\) and \(v\) be as in video, then we can describe the surface as: \[ \begin{aligned} &F(u,v)=(x,y,z,w)\qquad 0\leq u<2\pi ,0\leq v<2\pi \\ &x(u,v)=(r\cos v+a)\cos u \\ &y(u,v)=(r\cos v+a)\sin u \\ &z(u,v)=r\sin v \cos \frac{u}{2}\\ &w(u,v)=r\sin v\sin \frac{u}{2} \end{aligned} \] via simple calculus we know that circ \(S\) keeps its shape of a circle in \(\mathbf{R}^4\);however, when projected into \(\mathbf{R}^3\), the shape is broken and the surface even has self-intersection; to see that we notice that when \(u=\pi\) , \(\operatorname{proj}\circ F(\pi,2\pi-v)=\operatorname{proj}\circ F(\pi,v)=(-(r\cos v+a),0,0)\) and circle is twisted into the line \([-a-r,-a+r]\).

Let’s show that the Klein bottle is indeed a differentiable manifold.

Proof. Let \(U_1=\{(u,v)\in\mathbf{R}^2:0<u<2\pi,0<v<2\pi\}\) and define a map \(f_1=U_1 \rightarrow \mathbf{R}^4\) by \[ f_1(u,v)= \begin{cases} &x= (r\cos v+a)\cos u \\ &y=(r\cos v+a)\sin u \\ &z=r\sin v \cos \frac{u}{2}\\ &w=r\sin v\sin \frac{u}{2} \end{cases} \] It’s clear that \(f_1(U_1)\) contains the points of the Klein bottle that are not on the circs \(u=0\) and \(v=0\). We claim that \(f_1\) is injective.

To this end, let us first assume that \(z\neq 0\). Then both \(\sin v\) and \(\cos \frac{u}{2}\) are nonzero. Since \(0<u/2<\pi\), the expression \(\frac{w}{z}=\tan \frac{u}{2}\) determines \(u\). By knowing \(u\), we can use that \[ \sin v=\frac{w}{r\sin \frac{u}{2}},\quad \cos v=\frac{\frac{y}{\sin u}-a}{r} \] to determine \(v\). If \(z=0\), then \(v=\pi\) or \(u=\pi\), and the injectivity is easily checked.

It can be shown that if we we change the origin of \(u\) and the origin of \(v\), we can cover the whole Klein bottle by the images of maps similar to the above. For instance, if we define a map \(f_2:U_2\rightarrow \mathbf{R}^4\) given by \[ f_2= \begin{cases} &x=-(r\cos v^\prime+a)\sin u^\prime \\ &y=(r\cos v^\prime+a)\cos u^\prime \\ &z=r\sin v^\prime \cos (\frac{u^\prime}{2}+\frac{\pi}{4})\\ &w=r\sin v^\prime\sin (\frac{u^\prime}{2}+\frac{\pi}{4})\\ \end{cases} \qquad 0<u^\prime,v^\prime<2\pi \] Geometrically this means that we measure \(u^\prime\) from \(Oy\); we see that \(f_2(U_2)\) induces the points of the Klein bottle with \(u=0\) and that \(f_2\) is injective. Notice that \(f_1(U_1)\cap f_2(U_2)=W\) is not connected but has two connected components: \[ W_1=\{f_1(u,v):\frac{\pi}{2}<u<2\pi,0<v<2\pi\},\, W_2=\{f_1(u,v):0<u<\frac{\pi}{2},0<v<2\pi\} \] The change of coordinates is given by: \[ \begin{aligned} &f_2^{-1}\circ f_1\,(u,v)= \begin{cases} &u^\prime=u-\frac{\pi}{2}\\ &v^\prime=v \end{cases}\qquad \text{in } f^{-1}(W_1) \\ &f_2^{-1}\circ f_1(u,v)= \begin{cases} &u^\prime=u+\frac{3\pi}{2} \\ &v^\prime=2\pi-v \end{cases}\qquad \text{in } f^{-1}(W_2) \end{aligned} \] which is clearly differentiable;

In a similar way, we can find an injective map \(f_3:U_3\rightarrow \mathbf{R}^4\) which induces the points with \(v=0\); for instance: \[ f_3= \begin{cases} &x=(-r\sin v^{\prime\prime}+a)\cos u^{\prime\prime} \\ &y=(-r\sin v^{\prime\prime}+a)\sin u^{\prime\prime} \\ &z=r\cos v^{\prime\prime} \cos \frac{u^{\prime\prime}}{2}\\ &w=r\cos v^{\prime\prime} \sin \frac{u^{\prime\prime}}{2}\\ \end{cases} \qquad 0<u^{\prime\prime},v^{\prime\prime}<2\pi \].

It is also not difficult to check that the changes of coordinates are differentiable and this shows that \(\{(U_i,f_i)\}\) is a differentiable structure on the Klein bottle.

Remark. The Klein bottle (projected in \(\mathbf{R}^3\)) can be thought of as a twisted torus in the following sense (like the video above): The torus is obtained from a rectangle by identifying the opposite sides. In the Klein bottle, one of the sides is reflected across its center before performing the identification. Indeed the model in this case is another projection of Klein bottle which looks more like a real bottle. And it’s not a regular surface in \(\mathbf{R}^3\) for it has obvious self-intersections on this model.
