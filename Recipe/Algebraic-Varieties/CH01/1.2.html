We shall assume the field \(k\) is algebraically closed in this note.

Let \(X\) and \(Y\) be spaces with functions. The global effect of pull-back defines a mapping

\[ *: Morphism(X,Y) \to k \text{-}Alg \text{-} Hom_{k}(\mathcal{O}(Y),\mathcal{O}(X)) \]

ImportantDefintion

Affine Variety

Provided that \(\mathcal{O}(Y)\) is a finitely generated \(k\)-algebra, a space with functions \(Y\) is said to be an affine variety if the mapping \(*\) defined above is bijective for every space with functions \(X\).

Algebraic Variety

An algebraic variety \(X\) is defined to be a space with functions \(X\) which has a finite open covering \(U_{1},\dots,U_{n}\) where each \(U_{i}\) is affine. (Recall that an open subset of a space with functions carries the structure of space with functions naturally)

Morphism

A morphism between algebraic varieties is just a morphism of spaces with functions.

Next we give the first examples of varieties: the affine line \(\mathbb{A}^{1}\) and the projective line \(\mathbb{P}^{1}\).

NoteExample

\(\mathbb{A}^{1}\)

The underlying set of \(\mathbb{A}^{1}\) is just the field \(k\).

The topology of it is the cofinite topology, i.e. the closed subsets of \(\mathbb{A}^{1}\) are the whole set and the finite subsets.

The regular functions on open set \(U\): if \(U\) is empty, \(\mathcal{O}(U)=\{0\}\). Otherwise, if \(U=\mathbb{A}^{1}-\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\}\) then \(\mathcal{O}(U)\) consists of the rational functions \(g\) such that \(g\) has no poles anywhere in \(U\); i.e. \(g\) admits a representation: \[ U\to k, x \mapsto \frac{p(x)}{\prod(x-x_{i})^{m_{i}}} \ \text{where $p$ is a polynomial and the $m_{i}$ non-negative integers.} \]

In particular, \(\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{A}^{1}) \cong k[T]\) is (isomorphic to) a polynomial ring in one variable.

NoteExercise

Verify that \(\mathbb{A}^{1}\) is a space with functions.

TipProof
  1. Suppose that open set \(U=k-\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\}\) is the union of a family of open subsets \(U_{\alpha}\) where \(U_{\alpha}=k-\{x_{\alpha_{1}},\dots,x_{\alpha_{k_{\alpha}}}\}\) where \(k_{\alpha}\) is finite for each \(\alpha\) and \(\bigcap{\{x_{\alpha_{1}},\dots,x_{\alpha_{k_{\alpha}}}\}=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\}}\). If \(f\) is a regular function on \(U\) then \(f\) admits a representation of \(\frac{p(x)}{\prod_{i=1}^{n}(x-x_{i})^{m_{i}}}, m_{i} \in \mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\), then clearly \(f\) adimits a form \(\frac{p(x)}{\prod_{i=1}^{n}(x-x_{i})^{m_{i}}}\) on \(U_{\alpha}\) since \(U_{\alpha} \subseteq U\) and \(f \restriction_{U_{\alpha}} \in \mathcal{O}(U_{\alpha})\) since \(\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\} \subseteq\{x_{\alpha_{1}},\dots,x_{\alpha_{k_{\alpha}}}\}\).

  2. Given any two open sets \(U_1,U_2\), \(U_1 \cap U_2\) is an infinite set since \(k\) is infinite.

Conversely, if \(f:U \to k\) takes the form of \(\frac{p_{\alpha}(x)}{q_{\alpha}(x)}\) (as we observed) on each \(U_{\alpha}\), then clearly \(\frac{p_{\alpha}(x)}{q_{\alpha}(x)}=\frac{p_{\beta}(x)}{q_{\beta}(x)}\) on \(U_{\alpha} \cap U_{\beta}\) since they are all restricted by \(f\) which implies that \(p_{\alpha}(x){q_{\beta}(x)}-{p_{\beta}(x)}{q_{\alpha}(x)}\) has infinitely many roots for observation 2. It follows that \(\frac{p_{\alpha}(x)}{q_{\alpha}(x)}=\frac{p_{\beta}(x)}{q_{\beta}(x)}\) on the whole \(U\) for any \(\alpha,\beta\), and thus the global function \(f\) admits a representation \(\frac{p(x)}{q(x)} \in k(x)\).

It follows that \(p(x)q_{\alpha_{0}}(x)=p_{\alpha_{0}}(x)q(x)\) which implies that \(q_{\alpha_{0}}(x_0)=0\). Contradiction with \(q_{\alpha_0}\) vanishes nowhere in \(U_{\alpha_0}\). Hence \(q(x)=c\prod(x-t_i)^{s_i},t_i \in k-U\) and we get the form \(\frac{1}{c}p(x)/\prod(x-t_i)^{s_i},t_i \in k-U\) as we expected.

And \(\frac{1}{g(x)}=\frac{\prod(x-x_i)^{m_i}}{(x-r_{j})^{k_j}}\in D(g)\) where \(p\) splits into \(\prod(x-r_{j})^{k_j}\) in \(k\) since \(k\) is algebraically closed.

This is a technical and detailed proof. We may need the following elementary result (use Bézout theorem to verify): \[ (f, g) = 1 \text{ in } k[x] \Longleftrightarrow (f, g) = 1 \text{ in } K[x] \ \forall K/k \Longleftrightarrow \nexists \alpha \in \bar{k} : f(\alpha) = g(\alpha) = 0. \]

Next we show that \(\mathbb{A}^{1}\) is indeed an affine variety.

Let \(Y\) be any space with functions. A mapping \(f:Y \to \mathbb{A}^{1}=k\) is just a \(k\)-valued function.

ImportantClaim

\(f\) is a morphism if and only if \(f\) is regular on \(Y\).

NoteProof

“Only if”: The coordinate function \(x:k\to k; t \mapsto t\) is regular on \(\mathbb{A}^1\) and then \(f(x)\) is regular on \(Y\) since \(f\) is a morphism and \(f^{-1}(k)=Y\).

“if”: Suppose that \(f\) is regular on \(Y\). \(f\) is continuous since \(f^{-1}(\{t_1,\dots,t_n\})=Y-D(F)\), where \(F=\prod_{i=1}^{n} (f-t_{i})\), is the complement of an open set \(D(f)\). To show that \(f\) pulls back regualr function \(g=\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}\) on \(U=\mathbb{A}^1-\{t_1,\dots,t_n\}\), we notice that: \[ g \circ f=\frac{p(f(y))}{q(f(y))}=\frac{p(f(y))}{\prod_{i=1}^{n} (f(y)-t_i)^{m_{i}}} \] which is regular on \(D(f)=f^{-1}(k-\{t_1,\dots,t_n\})\) by definition.

At last we obtain that \(\mathbb{A}^{1}\) is affine: \[ *:Morphism(Y,\mathbb{A}^{1}) \cong \mathcal{O}(Y) \cong k \text{-} Alg \text{-} Hom (\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{A}^1),\mathcal{O}(Y)) \] The last equality holds for that \(\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{A}^1)=k[T]\) and the \(k\)-algebra maps are totally determined by the regular functions on \(Y\) as the images of \(T\).

ImportantExample

As a set \(\mathbb{P}^{1}=k \cup \{\infty\}\) where \(\infty\) is a set disjoint from \(\mathbb{P}^{1}\).

Topolgy:The closed subsets are finite sets, i.e. the open sets are like \(\mathbb{P}^{1}-\{x_1,\dots,x_n\}\).

Regular functions: The regular functions \(g\in \mathcal{O}(\mathbb{P}^1-\{x_1,\dots,x_n\})\) admit a form of rational functions without poles except at \(x_1,\dots,x_n\) where \(g\) has no poles at \(\infty\) is equvalent to saying \(g(\frac{1}{y})\) has no pole at \(y=0\).

It is a space with functions.

To see that it is indeed a variety, it has an open covering \((\mathbb{P}^{1}-\{\infty\})\cup (\mathbb{P}^{1}-\{0\})\) isomorphic to two affine lines: \[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{P}^{1}-\{\infty\} & \xrightarrow[Id]{\simeq} \mathbb{A}^1 \\ \mathbb{P}^{1}-\{0\} & \xrightarrow[\frac{1}{x}]{\simeq} \mathbb{A}^1 \quad \text{where} \frac{1}{\infty}=0 \end{aligned} \]

NoteExercise

Verify that \(\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{P}^{1})=k\)

TipProof

Suppose that \(f \in \mathcal{O}(\mathbb{P}^{1})\), then \(f \restriction_{(\mathbb{P}^{1}-\{\infty\})\cap(\mathbb{P}^{1}-\{0\})=k^{\times}}\) admits a representation

\(f \restriction_{(\mathbb{P}^{1}-\{\infty\})}\) admits a representation \(\frac{p_{1}(x)}{q_{1}(x)}\) where \(deg(p_{1}(x)) \leq deg(q_{1}(x)) \quad \text{or} \quad =\text{constant}\) and $ f _{(^{1}-{})}$ admits a representation \(p_{2}(x)\). Therefore, \(\frac{p_{1}(x)}{q_{1}(x)}=p_{2}(x)\) or \(p_{2}(x)=\text{constant}\) on \((\mathbb{P}^{1}-\{\infty\})\cap(\mathbb{P}^{1}-\{0\})=k^{\times}\) which only happens when \(f \restriction_{(\mathbb{P}^{1}-\{\infty\})}\) and \(f \restriction_{(\mathbb{P}^{1}-\{0\})}\) are constant. Hence \(f \in \mathcal{O}(\mathbb{P}^{1})\) if and only if \(f=\text{constant}\in k\).

NoteExercise

Show that \(\mathbb{P}^{1}\) is not affine.

TipProof

Suppose not, \(\mathbb{P}^{1}\) is an affine variety. By definition, for the field \({k}\) we have \(Mor(\mathbb{A}^{1},\mathbb{P}^{1}) \cong Hom_{k}(\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{P}^{1}),\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{A}^{1})) \cong Hom_{k}(k,k[T])\). But the latter corresponds to the ideals (\(0,k\)) of \(k\) which contradicts the fact that the first has at least two nontrivial morphisms (\(0,\mathbb{A}^{1} \to \mathbb{P}^{1}-\{0\},\mathbb{A}^{1} \to \mathbb{P}^{1}-\{\infty\}\)).

NoteExercise

Let \(X\) and \(Y\) be two affine varieties. Show that \(X \cong Y\) as spaces with functions if and only if \(\mathcal{O}(X) \cong \mathcal{O}(Y)\) as \(k\)-algebras.

TipProof

Suppose that \(X \cong Y\), then for some morphism \(g:X \to Y\), we have \(f: Y \to X\) such that \(f\circ g=id_{X}\) and \(g \circ f=id_{Y}\). Then \(g^{*} \circ f^{*}=id_{\mathcal{O}(X)}\) and \(f^{*} \circ g^{*}=id_{\mathcal{O}(Y)}\) where \(g^{*}:\mathcal{O}(Y) \to \mathcal{O}(X)\) and \(f^{*}:\mathcal{O}(X) \to \mathcal{O}(Y)\).

Conversely we suppose that \(X,Y\) are affine varieties and as \(k\)-algebras \(\mathcal{O}(X) \cong \mathcal{O}(Y)\). Then for some map \(G:\mathcal{O}(Y) \to \mathcal{O}(X)\) we have \(F:\mathcal{O}(X) \to \mathcal{O}(Y)\) such that \(F \circ G =id_{\mathcal{O}(Y)}, G \circ F =id_{\mathcal{O}(X)}\). Since \(X,Y\) are affine varieties (\(*\) is bijective), there exist unique morphisms \(g:X \to Y\) and \(f: Y \to X\) such that \(g^{*}=G,f^{*}=F\) and thus \(g\circ f=id_{Y},f\circ g=id_{X}\).
