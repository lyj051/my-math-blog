The Nullstellensatz

Algebraic Geometry
Preliminary
Algebraic Varieties by G.Kempf
Author

Eugene Lee

Published

August 7, 2026

The objective of this note is to prove the Hilbert’s Nullstellensatz which says that \(\operatorname{Spec} A\) has enough points so that the following morphism is injective.

\[ \phi:A \rightarrow \mathcal{O}(\Spec A) \]

Warning

In this theorem, we need to impose that 1. base field \(k\) is algebracially closed 2. \(A\) is a finitely generated \(k\)-algebra without nilpotents

For several statements in this note, the imposed condition turns out to be superfluous. Nevertheless, we should still clarify the prerequisites of Nullstellensatz and resist the temptation to generalize it in certain directions, so as to concentrate our attention on the main theorem.

To the end, we need few technical lemmas from commutative algebra as follows.

ImportantTheorem

Noether’s Normalization Lemma

Let \(k\) be a field and \(A\) be a non-zero finitely-generated \(k\)-algebra. Then there exists an injection \(B\xrightarrow{\subset}A\) where \(B\) is a polynomial ring \(k[X_1,\dots,X_d]\) i.e there exist \(d\geq0\) algebraically independent elements \(x_1,\dots,x_d \in A\), such that \(A\) is a finitely generated \(B\)-module.

Preparation:

Suppose that \(B \subseteq A\) are rings and \(A\) is finitely generated as \(B\)-algebra. Then we have: \[ A \ \text{is integral over} \ B \iff A \ \text{is finitely generated as} \ B \text{-module} \]

NoteProof

this proof is from Andrea Ferretti’s Commutative Algebra

Let \(y_1,\dots,y_m\) be a minimal set of generators of \(A\). We argue by induction over \(m\), the base case \(m=0,1\) is trivial.

If \(y_1,\dots,y_m\) are already algebracially independent then there is nothing to prove (just take \(B=A\) which is obviously finite), so assume that there is some polynomial \(f\) over \(k\) such that \(f(y_1,\dots,y_m)=0\). (wlog. we can also assume that \(y_{i}\) is not nilpotent) We can try to alter the set of generators and use the relation given by \(f\) to write one of the generators in terms of the other ones.

We perform the change of variable \[ z_i=y_i-y_1^{r^{i-1}} \] for \(i\geq 2\), where \(r\) to be chosen later. This is clearly an invertible change of variable which has the effect of increasing the degree of \(y_1\) inside \(f\). More precisely, in the equation \[ f(y_1,z_2+y_1^{r},\dots,z_m+y_1^{r^{m-1}})=0 \] we can look at the exponent of \(y_1\) in the various monomials. For a monomial of multidegree \((\alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_m)\) (in the \(y_{i}\)), the highest exponent of \(y_1\) in \(c_{(\alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_m)}y_{1}^{\alpha_1} \dots (z_{m}+y_1^{r^{m-1}})^{\alpha_{m}}\) after the change of variables is \[ \alpha_1+\alpha_2r+\dots+\alpha_mr^{m-1} \] And the corresponding term is \[ c_{(\alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_m)}y_{1}^{\alpha_1+\alpha_2r+ \dots +\alpha_{m}r^{m-1}} \] Suppose that \[ M=\max{\{c_{(\alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_m)}y_1^{\alpha_1}\dots y_m^{\alpha_m} \ \text{is a monomial in} \ f :(\alpha_1+\alpha_2r+ \dots +\alpha_{m}r^{m-1})\}} \] Then we collect all the terms like \(cy_1^{M}\) : \[ M=\alpha_1^{1}+\alpha_2^{1}r+ \dots +\alpha_{m}^{1}r^{m-1}=\dots=\alpha_1^{t}+\alpha_2^{t}r+ \dots +\alpha_{m}^{t}r^{m-1} \]

Then we get the term: \[ (\sum_{i=1}^{t} c_{(\alpha_1^{i},\dots,\alpha_m^{i})}) y_{1}^{M} \]

In fact, there is some trouble: \[ \sum_{i=1}^{t} c_{(\alpha_1^{i},\dots,\alpha_m^{i})} \] might vanish. But we can fix the trouble easily:

For \(r\) big enough (such that \(t=1\)), we see that this single monomial which will be of maximum degree for \(y_1\) such that the \(z_i\) do not appear in this monomial. This means that the transformed equation shows that \(y_1\) is integral over \(B:=k[z_1,\dots,z_m]\).

By induction, \(B\) already has the desired form of an integral extension of a polynomial ring and the thesis follows.

When \(k\) is infinite one can use a linear change of variables in the above proof. The only thing that is needed is that in the resulting polynomial relation, \(y_1\) appears with maximum degree in a monomial that contains no other indeterminates.

Consider a change of variables of the form: \[ z_i=y_i-\lambda_iy_1 \] This change of variables preserves the total degree of monomials, so consider the homogeneous part of maximum degree of \(f\), call it \(F\). We want to ensure that in \(F\) there appears a monomial containing only \(y_{1}\). Let \(t\) be the degree of \(F\).

The coefficient of \(y_{1}^{t}\) in \[ F(y_{1},z_{2}+\lambda_{2}y_{1},\dots,z_{m}+\lambda_{m}y_{1}) \] is a nonzero polynomial in \(\lambda_2,\dots,\lambda_m\). Since \(k\) is infinite, there exists a choice of \(\lambda_2,\dots,\lambda_m\) such that the coefficient don’t vanish, which is enough to show that \(y_{1}\) is integral over \(k[z_2,\dots,z_m]\).

ImportantTheorem

Assume that \(k\) is an algebracially closed field. Let \(k \subseteq B \subseteq A\) be \(k\)-algebras such that \(A\) is a finitely generated \(B\)-module. Then composition gives a surjection \[ Hom_{k}(A,k) \rightarrow Hom_{k}(B,k) \]

Preparation:

  1. \(A\hookrightarrow B\), \(I\unlhd A\), denote \(I \cdot B\) by \(I^{e}\); \(J\unlhd B\),denote \(J \cap A \unlhd A\) by \(J^{c}\), then we have \[ I^{ec}\supseteq I,J^{ce}\subseteq J \]

Hence we have \(A/I\hookrightarrow B/I^e\).

  1. Let \(I\unlhd \mathfrak{M} \unlhd R\) where \(\mathfrak{M}\) is a maximal ideal. Then we have \(R/I \hookrightarrow R/\mathfrak{M}\).

  2. Let \(A \in R^{n \times n}\) over a commutative ring \(R\). The adjugate \(\operatorname{adj}(A)\) is defined by:

\[ [\operatorname{adj}(A)]_{ij} = (-1)^{i+j} \det(A_{ji}) \]

Then:

\[ A \cdot \operatorname{adj}(A) = \operatorname{adj}(A) \cdot A = \det(A) \cdot I_n \]

If \(\det(A)\) is a unit in \(R\), then:

\[ A^{-1} = \frac{\operatorname{adj}(A)}{\det(A)} \]

NoteProof

Let \(\psi :B \rightarrow k\) be a \(k\)-algebra homomorphism. Let \(m\) be the kernel of \(\psi\). Consider the ideal \(mA\) in \(A\). If \(mA \neq A\), we can find a maximal ideal \(n\) of \(A\) such that \(mA \subseteq n\). Then \(A/n\) is a finite field extension of \(B/m=k\), for (by preparation) \[ k \cong B/m\hookrightarrow A/m^{e}\hookrightarrow A/n \]

Thus \(A/n=k\) as \(k\) is algebraically closed and the quotient mapping \(A \rightarrow k\) is an extension of \(\psi\) i.e. the diagram commutes

It remains to show that \(mA=A\) is impossible. Let \(A=B^{r}/R\) as a \(B\)-module where \(R\) is a \(B\)-submodule of \(B^{r}\) where \(r\) is finite. Let \(e_1,\dots,e_r\) be the unit vectors in \(B^r\) and we have \(\bar{e_1},\dots,\bar{e_r}\) generates \(A=mA\). Then for \(1 \leq i \leq r\), it follows that \(\bar{e_i}=\sum_{j=1}^{t}m_{j}^{i}(\sum_{l=1}^{r}b_{l}^{i}\bar{e_l})=\), thus we can write \(e_i=\sum_{k=1}^{r}m_{k}^{i}e_{k}+r_{i},m_{k}^{i}\in m,r_{i}\in R\). We have \[ \begin{bmatrix} b_1^1 & \dots & b_r^1 \\ \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ b_r^1 & \dots & b_r^r \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} e_1 \\ \vdots \\ e_r \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} r_1 \\ \vdots \\ r_r \end{bmatrix} \] where \[ b_{u}^{v}= \begin{cases} 1-m_u^v, & \text{if } u=v \\ -m_u^v, & \text{if } u \neq \end{cases} \]

And hence, by easy calculus, \[ det(b_u^v)\equiv 1_B (\operatorname{mod}m) \]

By the following lemma (called Cramer’s rule on the textbook): \[ \operatorname{adj}(M)\cdot M =M\cdot\operatorname{adj}(M) =\operatorname{det}(M)\cdot I_n \]

It follows that \(det(b_u^v)e_i\) is a linear combination of \(r_j\), and thus \(det(b_u^v)A=0\). In particular, \(det(b_u^v)\cdot1_A=0\) in \(A\) which implies that \(det(b_u^v)=0\) in \(A\) and also in \(B\). This is a contradiction to the fact that \(det(b_u^v)\equiv 1_B (\operatorname{mod}m)\) since \(0 \notin m\).

Now it’s not hard to prove the Nullstellensatz i.e. \[ \phi:A \rightarrow \mathcal{O}(\Spec A) \] is injective for finitely generated \(k\)-algebra \(A\)(\(k\) is algebraically closed) without nilpotents.

TipProof of Nullstellensatz

Let \(a\) be a non-zero element of \(A\). Consider the localization \(A_{(a)}\). This is non-zero since \(A\) has no nilpotents. In fact, by the exercise in the last section, \(A_{(a)}\) is also \(k\)-f.g (and reduced). Thus by the Noether’s Normalization Lemma, we have an inclusion \(k[X_1,\dots,X_r]\subseteq A_{(a)}\).

We need to find a point \(x\) of \(\Spec A\) such that \(\phi(a)(x) \equiv x(a)\) is non-zero where \(x: A \rightarrow k\) is a \(k\)-algebra homomorphism (this means non-zero element \(a\) cannot be sent to a non-zero function on \(\Spec A\) by \(\phi\)).

Let \(z:k[X_1,\dots,X_r] \rightarrow k\) be any \(k\)-algebra homomorphism (it clearly always exists). By the second lemma, we may lift (by composition) \(z\) to a \(k\)-algebra homomorphism \(y:A_{(a)} \rightarrow k\). Let \(x\) be the composition \(A \rightarrow A_{(a)} \rightarrow k\). Then \[ 1_k=x(1_A)=y(1_A/1_A)=y(a/a)=y(a/1_A)y(1_A/a)=x(a)y(1_A/a) \]

Therefore \(x(a)\) is non-zero. This proves the Nullstellensatz.

We use the trick called determinant trick again in the next famous and useful lemma:

NoteExercise.Nakayama’s Lemma

Let \(M\) be a f.g module over a commutative ring \(A\). Assume that there is an ideal \(I \unlhd A\) such that \(M=I \cdot M\). Then there is an element \(a\) of \(1+I\) such that \(aM=\{0\}\).

TipProof

Suppose that \(M=A[x_1,\dots,x_n]\). Then we have (totally same with the second lemma) \[ x_j=\sum_{k=1}^{n}y_k^jx_k \Rightarrow \sum_{k\neq j}(-y_k^j)x_k+(1-y_j^j)x_j=0 \]

Then we write it in the form of matrix: \[ (I_n-M)\vec{x}=0 \Rightarrow \operatorname{det}(I_n-M)\vec{x}=0 \]

And we know that \(\operatorname{det}(I_n-M)\equiv 1 (\operatorname{mod}I)\), then take \(a=\operatorname{det}(I_n-M)\).

Henceforth, by Nullstellensatz, we shall identify \(A\) with a ring of regular functions, as \(a \neq b \in A \Rightarrow \phi(a) \neq \phi(b) \in \mathcal{o}(\Spec A)\). Next we will give a reformulation of the Nullstellensatz which resembles Hilbert’s original statement.

ImportantTheorem

Suppose that \(k\) is algebraically closed and \(A\) is a f.g. \(k\)-algebra without nilpotents. If \(I\) is an ideal of \(A\), \[ \{a\in A: a(x)=0 \ \text{for all} \ x \in \ \text{zeroes}(I)\}=\sqrt{I} \] where \(\sqrt{I}=\{a \in A: a^n \in I \ \text{for some} \ n>0\}\).

NoteProof

One checks that \(\sqrt{I}\) is an ideal. Then \(A^\prime=A/\sqrt{I}\) is a f.g. \(k\)-algebra which has no nilpotents. Let \(b\) be an element of \(A-\sqrt{I}\), then \(b^\prime=b+\sqrt{I}\) is a non-zero element of \(A^\prime\). Hence there exists a homomorphism \(x^\prime:A^\prime \rightarrow k\) such that \(x^\prime(b^\prime) \neq 0\). Let \(x:A \rightarrow A^\prime \xrightarrow{x^\prime} k\) be the composition. Then by construction \(x\) is a point of \(zeroes(I)\) and \(b(x) \neq 0\). This proves the desired inclusion “\(\subseteq\)”. The reverse inclusion is trivial because \(b^n(x)=0 \iff b(x)=0 \ \text{if } n >0\).

Some special cases will be useful.

NoteCorollary

Given a subset \(J\) of \(A\) then \(zeroes(J)=\emptyset \iff 1=\sum_{\text{finite}}a_kj_k \ \text{where } a_k \in A, j_k \in J\).

TipProof

Set \(I=JA\). Hence if \(\text{zeroes}(J)=\emptyset\), then \(\text{zereos}(I)=\emptyset\). Then \[ \{a\in A: a(x)=0 \ \text{for all} \ x \in \ \text{zeroes}(I)\}=A=\sqrt{I} \] i.e. \(1=1^n \in JA\) which is the second statement. The converse is obvious.

NoteCorollary

Let \(f_{i}\) be elements of \(A\) and \(n_i\) be any positive integers. Then there exist \(a_i\) such that: \[ \Spec A=\bigcup D(f_i) \iff 1=\sum_{\text{finite}} a_if_i \]

TipProof

\[ \Spec A=\bigcup D(f_i) \iff \emptyset=\bigcap{D(f_i)^{c}}=\bigcap{\operatorname{zeroes}(f_i)}=\bigcap{\operatorname{zeroes}(f_i^{n_i})}=\operatorname{zeroes}(\{f_i^{n_i}\}) \]

Then by the corollary above (take \(\{f_i^{n_i}\}\) as \(J\)) it’s equivalent to \(1=\sum_{\text{finite}}a_if_i^{n_i}\) for some \(a_i \in A\).

This result has an interesting topological consequence.

ImportantTheorem

\(\Spec A\) is quasi-compact.

NoteProof

By the exercise in the last section \(D(f_i)\) is a basis for the topology of \(\Spec A\). If \(\Spec A=\bigcup {D(f_i)}\) is an open cover by this then \(1=\sum_{J \text{ is finite}}a_jf_j\) for some \(a_j \in A\). Then \(\Spec A= \bigcup_{j \in J}D(f_j)\) is a finite subcover.
