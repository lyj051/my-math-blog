G-ideals, Hilbert rings, and the Nullstellensatz

Author

Ian Lee

Published

October 1, 2026

A modern style: the first thing to do with a maximal ideal \(\mathfrak{m}\) in \(R[x]\) is to contract it back to the ideal \(\mathfrak{m} \cap R\) in \(R\) where \(R\) is more or less aebitrary commutative ring. When we do this we certainly get a prime ideal in \(R\), but we wish it were a maximal ideal. A close examination of the facts leads to the sequence of ideals: G-domain, G-ideal, and Hilbert ring.

Theorem 1

Let \(R\) be an integral domain with quotient field \(K\). The following two statements are equivalent:

  1. \(K\) is a finitely generated \(R\)-algebra.

  2. as a ring, \(K\) can be generated over \(R\) by one element.

Proof. Of course, (b) implies (a). For the converse, let \(K=R[\frac{a_1}{b_1}, \ldots, \frac{a_n}{b_n}]\) where \(a_i, b_i \in R\) and \(b_i \neq 0\). Then \(K=R[\frac{1}{b_1 \cdots b_n}]\).

Definition 1

An integral domain satisfying either (hence both) of the statements in Theorem 1 is called a G-domain.

Remark. The name honors Oscar Goldman.

The next two theorems are simple but useful. Note that any G-domain has an element \(u\) of the type occurring in Theorem 2; note also that as a corollary of Theorem 2, in a G-domain the intersection of all non-zero prime ideals is non-zero.

Theorem 2

Let \(R\) be an integral domain with quotient field \(K\). For a non-zero element \(u\in R\) the following three statements are equivalent:

  1. Any non-zero prime ideal contains \(u\).

  2. Any non-zero ideal contains a power of \(u\).

  3. \(K=R[\frac{1}{u}]\).

Proof.

  1. implies (2): Let \(I\) be a non-zero ideal. If \(I\) contains no power of \(u\), then by lemma 1 \(I\) can be expanded to a prime ideal \(\mathfrak{p}\) disjoint from \(\{u^n\}\), a contradiction.

  2. implied (3): Take any \(b\neq 0\) in \(R\). By (2) there is a power \(u^n\in (b)\), so that \(\frac{1}{b}=\frac{r}{u^n}\in R[\frac{1}{u}]\) for some \(r\in R\).

  3. implies (1): Let \(\mathfrak{p}\) be a non-zero prime ideal. Then \(\mathfrak{p}\) contains a non-zero element \(a\). By (3) we have \(\frac{1}{a}=\frac{r}{u^n}\) for some \(r\in R\). It follows that \(u^n=ar\in \mathfrak{p}\), so that \(u\in \mathfrak{p}\).

Lemma 3

Let \(R\) be a G-domain with quotient field \(K\) and let \(T\) be a ring lying between \(R\) and \(K\). Then \(T\) is a G-domain.

Proof. If \(K=R[\frac{1}{u}]\), then \(K=T[\frac{1}{u}]\) all the more so.

Lemma 4

A unique factorization domain is a G-domain if and only if it has only a finite number of primes (up to units).

Proof. Let \(R\) be a UFD. Suppose that \(R\) is a G-domain then \(K=R[\frac{1}{u}]\) for some \(u\neq 0\). Write \(u=cp_1^{r_1}\ldots p_n^{r_n}\) where \(r\) is unit and \(p_i\)’s are primes. Hence if it has an infinite number of primes, we can pick a \(p\neq p_i\) such that \(\frac{1}{p_i}\neq \frac{a}{ru}\) for any \(a\in R\). The converse is clear.

To use the notion of a G-domain effectively, we need to know how it behaves under ring extensions. The next two theorems, together with exercise 1, give fairly complete information.

Theorem 5

If \(R\) is an integral domain and \(x\) is an indeterminate over \(R\), then \(R[x]\) is never a G-domain.

Proof. Let \(K\) be the quotient field of \(R\); if \(R[x]\) is a G-domain, so is \(K[x]\) since \(R[x]\subseteq K[x] \subseteq K(x)\). But \(K[x]\) is a principal ideal domain. So we will achieve our goal if we show that \(K[x]\) has an infinite number of primes. We can apply the Euclid’s trick. Suppose that \(p_1,\ldots,p_n\) are all the irreducible monic polynomials and form \(1+p_1\ldots p_n=q\). Then \(q\) is divisible by none of the \(p_i\)’s, a contradiction.

Theorem 6

Let \(R\subset T\) be integral domains and suppose that \(T\) is algebraic over \(R\) and finitely generated as a ring over \(R\). Then \(R\) is a G-domain if and only if \(T\) is a G-domain.

Proof. Let \(K\) and \(L\) be the quotient field of \(R\) and \(T\). Suppose first that \(R\) is a G-domain; say \(K=R[\frac{1}{u}]\) for some \(u\in R\). Then \(T[\frac{1}{u}]\) is a domain algebraic over \(R[\frac{1}{u}]=K\), hence is itself a field (by lemma 11), necessarily equal to \(L\). Thus \(L\) is a G-domain.

We assume that \(T\) is a G-domain, \(L=T[\cfrac{1}{v}]\) and \(T=R[w_1,\ldots,w_k]\). The elements \(\frac{1}{v},w_1,\ldots,w_k\) are algebraic over \(K\) and consequently satisfy equations with coefficients in \(R\) which lead off, say \[ \begin{aligned} av^{-m}+\cdots & =0 \\ b_iw_i^{n_i}+\cdots & =0 \qquad (i=1,\cdots,k) \end{aligned} \] Adjoin \(a^{-1},b_1^{-1},\cdots,b_k^{-1}\) to \(R\), obtaining a ring \(R_1\) between \(R\) and \(K\). The field \(L\) is generated over \(R\) by \(w_1,\cdots,w_k,v^{-1}\). A fortiori these elements generate \(L\) over \(R_1\). Now over \(R_1\) we have arranged that \(w_1,\cdots,w_k,v^{-1}\) are integral. Hence \(L\) is integral over \(R_1\). By lemma 12, \(R_1\) is a field and necessarily equal to \(K\). So \(K\) is a finitely generated ring over \(R\) and \(R\) is a G-domain, as required.

For convenience we add a theorem, which is immediate from the theorems above.

Theorem 7

Let \(R\) be a domain, \(u\) an element of a larger domain. If \(R[u]\) is a G-domain, then \(u\) is algebraic over \(R\) and \(R\) is a G-domain.

Proof. Theorem 5 and 6 apply.

So far there has been no sign that G-domains have anything to do with maximal ideals in polynomial rings. The next theorem exhibits the connection.

Theorem 8

An integral domain \(R\) is a G-domain if and only if there exists in the polynomial ring \(R[x]\) an ideal \(\mathfrak{m}\) which is maximal and satisfies \(\mathfrak{m}\cap R=\{0\}\).

Proof. Suppose that \(R\) is a G-domain, say \(K=R[u^{-1}]\) where \(K\) is the quotient field of \(R\). We can map \(R[x]\) homomorphically into \(K\) by sending \(x\) into \(u^{-1}\). The image is all of \(K\) so the kernel \(\mathfrak{m}\) is maxiaml. Since the homomorphism is one-to-one on \(R\), we have \(\mathfrak{m}\cap R=\{0\}\).

Conversely, let \(\mathfrak{m}\) be maxiaml in \(R[x]\) and satisfy \(\mathfrak{m}\cap R=\{0\}\). Denote the image of \(x\) in the natural homomorphism \(R[x]\rightarrow R[x]/\mathfrak{m}\) by \(v\). Then \(R[v]\) is a field. By theorem 7, \(R\) is a G-domain.

Definition 2

A prime ideal \(\mathfrak{p}\) in a commutative ring \(R\) is a G-ideal if \(R/\mathfrak{p}\) is a G-domain.

Theorem 9: Krull’s Theorem

The nilradical \(N\) of any commutative ring \(R\) is the intersection of all prime ideals in \(R\).

Theorem 10: strengthened Krull’s theorem

The nilradical \(N\) of any commutative ring \(R\) is the intersection of all G-ideals in \(R\).

Proof. Clearly a nilpotent element lies in every prime ideal, hence every G-ideal. Conversely, suppose \(u\notin N\). We must construct a G-ideal excluding \(u\). The ideal \(\{0\}\) is disjoint from \(\{u^n\}\); We can expand it to an ideal \(\mathfrak{p}\) maxiaml w.r.t disjointness from \(\{u^n\}\). We know that \(\mathfrak{p}\) is prime (Lemma 1). We show that \(\mathfrak{p}\) is a G-ideal. In the domain \(\overline{R}=R/\mathfrak{p}\), let \(\overline{u}\) denote the image of \(u\). Suppose that \(\overline{\mathfrak{q}}\) is a non-zero prime ideal in \(\overline{R}\). Assume that \(\overline{u}\notin \mathfrak{q}\). Then \(u\notin \mathfrak{q}\) and hence \(\mathfrak{q}\) is in the exclusion of \(\{u^n\}\). As \(\mathfrak{q}\supseteq \mathfrak{p}\) and \(\mathfrak{p}\) is maximal, it follows that \(\mathfrak{p}=\mathfrak{q}\) and then \(\overline{\mathfrak{q}}=0\), a contradiction. Therefore, every non-zero prime ideal in \(\overline{R}\) contains \(\overline{u}\). By Theorem 2, \(\overline{R}\) is a G-domain, and \(\mathfrak{p}\) is a G-ideal.

Corollary 11

Let \(I\) be any ideal in a commutative ring \(R\). Then the radical of \(I\) is the intersection of all G-ideals containing \(I\).

Proof. Notice that \(\sqrt{I}/I\) is the nilradical of \(R/I\) and then apply the strengthened Krull’s theorem.

Let \(I\) be any ideal in a ring \(R\). We write \(\overline{R}\) for the quotient ring \(R/I\). In the polynomial ring \(R[x]\) there is a smallest extension \(I\,R[x]\) of \(I\). The quotient ring \(R[x]/I\, R[x]\) is in a natural way isomophic to \(\overline{R}[x]\). In treating many problems, we can in this way reduce to the case \(I=\{0\}\), and we shall often do so.

Theorem 12

An ideal \(I\) in a ring \(R\) is a G-ideal if and only if it is the contraction of a maximal ideal in the polynomial ring \(R[x]\).

Proof. Assume that there is a maxiaml ideal \(\mathfrak{m}\trianglelefteq\overline{R}[x]\) such that \(\mathfrak{m}\cap \overline{R}=\{0\}\). Denote by \(\pi\) the canonical homomorphism \(R\rightarrow \overline{R}\). It follows that \(\pi^{-1}(\mathfrak{m}\cap\overline{R})=\pi^{-1}(\{0\})=I\). Define \(R[x]\twoheadrightarrow \overline{R}[x]\) by \(\sum a_i x^i \mapsto \sum \overline{a_i}x^i\) and denote it by \(\pi^\prime\) which is clearly an extension of \(\pi\). Then \((\pi^\prime)^{-1} (\mathfrak{m}\cap \overline{R})=\pi^{-1}(\mathfrak{m}\cap \overline{R})\) as \(\pi^\prime \equiv \pi\) on \(R\). Thus \(I=\pi^{-1}(\mathfrak{m})\cap R\). Via the natural isomorphism \(R[x]/I\, R[x]\cong \overline{R}[x]\), it follows that \(\pi^{-1}(\mathfrak{m})\) is a maximal ideal in \(R[x]\).

Therefore it suffices to show the theroem when \(I=\{0\}\) and Theorem 8 applies.

Theorem 13

Let \(\mathfrak{m}\) be a maxiaml ideal in \(R[x]\) and suppose that the contraction \(\mathfrak{m}\cap R=\mathfrak{n}\) is maxiaml in \(R\). Then \(\mathfrak{m}\) can be generated by \(\mathfrak{n}\) and one more element \(f\). We can select \(f\) to be a monic polynomial which maps \(\mod{\mathfrak{n}}\) into an irreducible polynomial over the field \(R/\mathfrak{n}\).

Proof. Since \(\overline{R}[x]\cong R[x]/\mathfrak{n}R[x]\), \(\overline{\mathfrak{m}}=(\overline{f})\) for some irreducible \(\overline{f}\in (R/\mathfrak{n})[x]\). Apply \(\pi^{-1}\). It follows that \(\mathfrak{m}=(f,\mathfrak{n})\). (Indeed we can generate any maxiaml ideal containing \(\mathfrak{n}R[x]\) in this way.)

Corollary 14

Suppose, in addition to the hypothesis of Theorem above, that \(R/\mathfrak{n}\) is algebraically closed. Then \(\mathfrak{m}=(\mathfrak{n},x-a)\) for some \(a\in R\).

Any maximal ideal is a G-ideal. The nice class of rings is where the reverse holds.

Definition 3

A commutative ring \(R\) is a Hilbert ring if every G-ideal in \(R\) is maximal. (Krull’s designation was Iacobson ring, and Bourbaki has followed this terminology.)

Lemma 15

Any homomorphic image of a Hilbert ring is a Hilbert ring.

Proof. Let \(R\) be a Hilbert ring and let \(\pi:R\rightarrow S\) be a surjective homomorphism. Let \(\mathfrak{p}\) be a G-ideal in \(S\). Then \(\pi^{-1}(\mathfrak{p})\) is a G-ideal in \(R\) and hence maximal. It follows that \(\mathfrak{p}\) is maximal in \(S\).

Theorem 16

In a Hilbert ring the radical of any ideal is the intersection of maximal ideals containing it.

Theorem 17

A commutative ring \(R\) is a Hilbert ring if and only if the polynomial ring \(R[x]\) is a Hilbert ring.

Proof. If \(R[x]\) is a Hilbert ring, then the homomorphic image \(R\) is also a Hilbert ring.

Conversely, suppose that \(R\) is a Hilbert ring. Let \(\mathfrak{m}\) be a G-ideal in \(R[x]\). Let \(\mathfrak{p}=\mathfrak{m}\cap R\) which is a prime ideal. Denote by \(\overline{R}[x]\) \((R/\mathfrak{p})[x]\) and by \(\overline{m}\) the image of \(\mathfrak{m}\). It suffices to show that \(\overline{\mathfrak{m}}\) is maxiaml. Consider the canonical homomorphism \(\overline{R}[x]\twoheadrightarrow \overline{R}[x]/\overline{\mathfrak{m}}\) and let \(u\) be the image of \(x\). Then \(\overline{R}[u]\) is a G-domain. It follows that \(u\) is algebraic over \(\overline{R}\) and \(\overline{R}\) is a G-domain. Then \(\overline{R}\) is a field since the G-ideal \(\{0\}\) is maximal. But this makes \(\overline{R}[u]=\overline{R}[x]/\overline{\mathfrak{m}}\) a field, proving \(\overline{\mathfrak{m}}\) to be maxiaml.

Theorem 18

Let \(K\) be an algebraically closed field and let \(x_1,\dots,x_n\) be indeterminates over \(K\). Then any maximal ideal in \(K[x_1,\dots,x_n]\) is of the form \((x_1-a_1,\dots,x_n-a_n)\), \(a_i\in K\); i.e. it consists of all polynomials vanishing at a point.

Proof. Let \(R=K[x_1,\dots,x_{n-1}]\). \(R\) is a Hilbert ring by iterated use of Theorem 17 . We are given a mamixal ideal \(\mathfrak{m}\) in \(R[x_n]\). By Theorem 12, \(\mathfrak{n}=\mathfrak{m}\cap R\) is a G-ideal in \(R\), and hence maximal. We make an induction on \(n\), assuming that \(\mathfrak{n}=(x_1-a_1,\dots,x_{n-1}-a_{n-1})\). Therefore \(R/\mathfrak{n}\cong K\) is algebraically closed. By Corollary 14, \(\mathfrak{m}=(\mathfrak{n},x_n-a_n)\) for some \(a_n\in K\).

Theorem 19

Let \(K\) be an algebraically closed field and \(f,g_1,\dots,g_r\) in \(n\) variables with coefficients in \(K\). Assume that \(f\) vanishes at all common zeros of \(g_1,\dots,g_r\). The some power of \(f\) lies in the ideal \((g_1,\dots,g_r)\).

Proof. By the theorem above, our hypothesis says that \(f\) lies in the intersection of all maxiaml ideals containing \((g_1,\dots,g_r)\). Since \(K[x_1,\dots,x_r]\) is a Hilbert ring; Theorem 16 is applicable and shows that \(f\) is in the radical of \((g_1,\dots,g_r)\).
