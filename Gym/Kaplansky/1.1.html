Exercise 1

Let \(R\) be a commutative ring where \(0\neq 1\). Suppose that every ideal \(\big(\neq (1)\big)\) is prime. Show that \(R\) is a field.

Proof. Clearly \(R\) is a domain. Let \(a\neq 0\in R\) be a non-unit. Then \((a^2)\) is a prime ideal and thus \(a^2\in(a^2)\) implies that \(a\in(a^2)\). It follows that there exists an element \(r\in R\) such that \(a=a^2r\). As \(R\) is a domain, we have \(1=ar\) which leads to contradiction.

Exercise 2

Let us say that a saturated multiplicatively closed set \(S\) is generated by \(\{x_i\}\) if \(S\) is the smallest such set containing the \(x\)’s. Prove that if \(S\) is finitely generated in this sense, then it can be generated by a single element.

Proof. Suppose that \(S\) is generated by \(x_1,\dots,x_n\); we show that \(S\) is also generated by \(x_1\dots x_n\);denote \(S^\prime\) the saturated multiplicatively closed set generated by this single element. \(S^\prime\subseteq S\) for \(S\) contains \(x_1\dots x_n\); \(S\subseteq S^\prime\) for \(S^\prime\) contains the \(x_i\)’s. Therefore \(S=S^\prime\).

Exercise 3

Let \(\mathfrak{p}\) be a finitely generated prime ideal with annihilator \(0\). Prove that the annihilator of the module \(\mathfrak{p}/\mathfrak{p}^2\) is \(\mathfrak{p}\).

Proof. Suppose that \(\mathfrak{p}\) is generated by \(x_1,\dots,x_r\). Clearly \(\mathfrak{p}\subseteq \operatorname{ann}(\mathfrak{p}/\mathfrak{p}^2)\); Let \(x\) be any element in \(\operatorname{ann}(\mathfrak{p}/\mathfrak{p}^2)\). The element in \(\mathfrak{p}^2\) admits the form \(\sum_{i=1}^{r}a_ip_i\) where \(a_i\in \mathfrak{p}\). Then it follows that \[ xp_i=\sum_{j=1}^{r}a_{ij}p_j,\qquad a_{ij}\in \mathfrak{p} \] for \(i=1,\dots,r\).

Write the linear relations betwen \(p_i\)’s in the form of matrix: \[ \begin{bmatrix} a_{11}-x & a_{12} & \ldots & a_{1r} \\ a_{21} & a_{22}-x &\ldots & a_{2r}\\ \vdots &\vdots &\ddots & \vdots \\ a_{r1}& a_{r2} &\ldots &a_{rr}-x \end{bmatrix} \cdot \begin{bmatrix} p_1 \\ p_2 \\ \vdots \\ p_r \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0\\ 0\\ \vdots\\ 0 \end{bmatrix} \] Take determinant of the matrix \(M\); Since \(\operatorname{ann}(\mathfrak{p})=0\) we get \(\det M=0\);i.e. there exists a polynomial \(f\) with coefficients in the prime ideal \(\mathfrak{p}\) such that \(f(x)=0\). By induction on the degree of \(f\) (there we need the ideal being prime), we have \(x\in \mathfrak{p}\).

The purpose of the exercise below is to show that in Ex.3 we cannot drop the requirement that \(\mathfrak{p}\) is prime.

Exercise 4

Let \(k\) be a field. Let \(R\) be the ring of polynomials in one indeterminant \(x\) over \(k\), subject to the condition that they contain no terms in \(x\) or \(x^2\). Let \(I\) be the ideal in \(R\) generated by \(x^3\) and \(x^4\). Prove: \(x^5\notin I\), and \(x^5I\subset I^2\); i.e. \(I\subsetneq\operatorname{ann}(I/I^2)\).

Proof. Assume that \(x^5=f(x)x^3+g(x)x^4\); then \(x^2=f(x)+g(x)x\) which contradicts the fact that \(f\) and \(g\) contain no terms in \(x\) or \(x^2\). So \(x^5\notin I\). Any element in \(x^5I\) admits the form \(x^8f(x)+x^9g(x)\). As \(x^8\in I^2\) and \(f(x)\in R\), \(x^8f(x)\in I^2\); As \(x^6\in I^2\) and \(x^3g(x)\in R\), \(x^9g(x)\in I^2\). Hence \(x^5I\subset I^2\).

Exercise 5

Let \(\mathfrak{p}=(p)\) be a principal prime ideal and \(J=\bigcap_{n=1}\mathfrak{p}^n\).

  1. If \(\mathfrak{q}\) is a prime ideal properly contained by \(\mathfrak{p}\), prove that \(\mathfrak{q}\subset J\).

  2. Assume further that \(p\) is a non-zero-divisor. Prove: \(J=pJ\).

  3. Again assuming that \(p\) is a non-zero-divisor, prove that \(J\) is prime.

  4. Assume that \(R\) is a domain and \(J\) is finitely generated. Prove that \(J=0\) and that there is no prime ideal properly between \(P\) and \(0\).

  5. Assume merely that \(J\) is finitely generated. Prove that there cannot exist a distinct chain \(\mathfrak{p}\supsetneq\mathfrak{q}_1\supsetneq \mathfrak{q}_2\) of prime ideals.

Proof.

  1. For any \(q\in \mathfrak{q}\), we show that \(q\in\mathfrak{p}^n\) for any \(n\) by induction; \(q\in\mathfrak{p}^1\) obviously; Assume \(q\in\mathfrak{p}^n=(p^n)\), then \(q=p^nq_1\) for some \(q_1\); As \(p^n\notin \mathfrak{q}\) (otherwise \(p\in \mathfrak{q}\)), we have \(q=p^{n+1}q_2\). By induction, it follows that \(\mathfrak{q}\subset J\).

  2. For any \(j\in J\), we can write \(j=px_1\) since \(j\in\mathfrak{p}\). As \(j\in (p^n)\) for any \(n\), we have \(p^nx_n=px_1\). Then \(x_1=p^{n-1}x_n\) for any \(n\) since \(p\) is a non-zero-divisor. Hence \(x_1\in J\) and hence \(J\subseteq pJ\). The reverse inclusion is clear.

  3. Suppose \(ab\in J\) with neither \(a\) nor \(b\) in \(J\). Since \(ab\in\mathfrak{p}\) which is prime ideal, we can assume that \(a\in\mathfrak{p}\). As \(a\notin \bigcap_{n=1} \mathfrak{p}^n\), there exists a positive integer \(m\) such that \(a\in \mathfrak{p}^m\) while \(a\notin\mathfrak{p}^{m+1}=p^m\mathfrak{p}\). So \(a\) has a form of \(a=p^ma_1\) where \(a_1\notin \mathfrak{p}\). Similarly \(b\) has the form of \(b=p^nb_1\) where \(b_1\notin \mathfrak{p}\) (here \(n\) could be zero). Then \(ab=p^{m+n}a_1b_1\) which is contained in \((p^{m+n+1})\). As \(p\) is a non-zero-divisor, \(a_1b_1=px\in\mathfrak{p}\). As \(\mathfrak{p}\) is a prime ideal, either \(a\) or \(b\) is in \(\mathfrak{p}\). We get a contradiction then.

  4. Using determinant trick, it follows that there exists \(x\in R\) with \(x\equiv 1 (\operatorname{mod} \mathfrak{p})\) such that \(xJ=0\). Then \(J=0\) as \(x\neq0\) and \(R\) is a domain. The final statement is immediate from part (a).

  5. Assume such a chain exists. Then we get a chain \(\mathfrak{p}\,\operatorname{mod}\mathfrak{q_2}\supsetneq \mathfrak{q_1}\,\operatorname{mod} \mathfrak{q_2}\supsetneq 0\) of prime ideals in the domain \(R/\mathfrak{q}_2\). This is impossible from part (d).

Exercise 6

Let \(\mathfrak{p}\) be a prime ideal in \(R\), \(I\) the ideal generated by all the idempotents in \(\mathfrak{p}\). Prove that \(R/I\) has no non-trivial idempotents. (This result is due to D.Lazard who studied under P.Samuel.)

Lemma 1

Let \(\{e_i\}_{i\in I}\) be some idempotents in \(R\) and \(I\) be the ideal generated by \(\{e_i\}_{i\in I}\). For any element \(x\in I\), there is an idempotent \(f\in I\) such that \(fx=x\).

Proof. Suppose that \(x=\sum_{i=1}^{r} r_ie_i\). It suffices to show that there exists an idempotent \(f\in I\) such that \(fe_i=e_i\) for \(i=1,\dots,r\). The expected \(f\) can be explicitly constructed as follows: \[ \begin{aligned} f&=\sum e_i - \sum e_ie_j + \dots +(-1)^{r-1}e_1\dots e_r.\\ &=\sum_{S\subseteq \{1,\dots,r\}}(-1)^{|S|-1}e_S \end{aligned} \] It’s clear that \(fe_k=e_k\) and hence \(f^2=f(\sum r_ie_i)=\sum r_ife_i=r_ie_i=f\).

Proof. Let \(e\in R/I\) be an idempotent. Pick \(u\in R\) such that \(u\,\operatorname{mod}I=e\). As \(e(1-e)=0\) in \(R/I\) it follows that \(u(1-u)\in I\); thus in \(\mathfrak{p}\). W.LO.G assume that \(u\in \mathfrak{p}\) since \(\mathfrak{p}\) is prime. By lemma above, we have \(f(u-u^2)=u-u^2\) for idempotent \(f\in I\). Then \([(1-f)u]^2=(1-f)u^2=(1-f)u\) and thus \((1-f)u\) is an idempotent in \(\mathfrak{p}\), hence in \(I\). As \(fu\in I\), \((1-f)u+fu=u\in I\). Thus \(u \,\operatorname{mod}I=0\) in \(R/I\) which is a trivial idempotent.

Exercise 7

Let \((p)\) and \((q)\) be non-zero principal prime ideals in a ring. Suppose that \((p)\subseteq (q)\) and that \(p\) is a non-zero-divisor. Prove that \((p)=(q)\).

Proof. It follows from \((p)\subseteq (q)\) that \(p=qr\) for some element \(r\in R\). As \(p\) is a prime element, either \(p|q\) or \(p|r\). If \(p|q\), then \(q=px\in(p)\) and the proof is complete; if \(p|r\), write \(r=pa\), then \(p(1-qa)=0\). Since \(p\) is a non-zero-divisor, \(1=qa\) which contradicts the fact that \(q\) is a prime element.

Exercise 8: A modernization of Euclid’s theorem on the infinitude of primes

Prove that an infinite integral domain \(R\) with a finite number of units has an infinite number of maximal ideals.

Proof. Notice that \((0)\) is not a maximal ideal as \((0)\subsetneq (a)\) for some \(a\notin R^\times\); Assume that \(R\) has a finite number of maximal ideals, say \(\mathfrak{m}_1,\dots,\mathfrak{m}_n\). Let \(a_i\in \mathfrak{m}_i\) be a non-zero element. Notice that any non-unit element is contained in one maximal ideal and thus the union of all maximal ideals. Since \((a_1\dots a_n+1)\notin \bigcup_{i}\mathfrak{m}_i\) (otherwise \(1\in\mathfrak{m}_i\) for some \(i\)), \((a_1\dots a_n+1)\) is a unit. Similarly all the elements in the form of \(ka_1\dots a_n+1, k\in\mathbb{N}\) are units. However, these elements are distinct for \(R\) is a domain. Hence \(R\) has infinitely many units which contradicts to the assumption that \(R\) has a finite number of units. The proof is then complete.

Exercise 9: A naive version of Gauss’s lemma

Let \(f\) and \(g\) be polynomials in an indeterminant over a ring \(R\). Suppose that the ideal generated by the coefficients of \(f\) is \(R\), and that the same is true for \(g\). Prove that the coefficients of \(fg\) also generate \(R\).

Proof. Assume that the coefficients of \(fg\) lie in a maximal ideal \(\mathfrak{m}\). Write \(f=a_nx^n+\dots+a_sx^s+\dots+a_0\) and \(g=b_mx^m+\dots+b_tx^t+\dots+b_0\) where \(a_s\) and \(b_t\) are the terms of highest degree which are not in \(\mathfrak{m}\). Consider the term of degree \(s+t\) in \(fg\). The coefficient of that term is \[ a_sb_t+\sum_{i>s}^{i+j=s+t}a_ib_j+\sum_{j>t}^{i+j=s+t}a_ib_j \] which does not lie in \(\mathfrak{m}\). Thus we get a contradiction.

Exercise 10

In a ring \(R\) let \(I\) be maximal among non-principal ideals. Prove that \(I\) is prime. (a result from the famous finite theorist M.Isaacs)

Proof. Let \(I\) be such a maximal ideal. Assume that it’s not a prime ideal. Thus there exist \(a,b\notin I\) with \(ab\in I\). Since \(I+(a)\supsetneq I\), \(I+(a)\) is a principal ideal, say \((c)\). Take \(J=I:(c)\). Clearly \(I\subset J\) and \(b\in J\). It follows that \(J\supsetneq I\) and then it’s a principal ideal. Clearly \(Jc\subseteq I\); To show the reverse inclusion, choose any element \(i\in I\subsetneq I+(a)=(c)\). Hence \(i=cr\) for some \(r\in R\). As \(cr\in I\), we have \(r\in J\). Hence \(i\in Jc\) and hence \(I=Jc\). However, \(Jc\) is a principal ideal. Consequently we get a contradiction.

Exercise 11

In a ring \(R\) let \(I\) be maximal among ideals that are not countably generated. Prove that \(I\) is prime.

Proof. Let \(I\) be such a maximal ideal. Assume that it’s not a prime ideal. Thus there exist \(a,b\notin I\) with \(ab\in I\). Since \(I+(a)\subsetneq I\), it’s a countably generated ideal, say \(I+(a)=\sum_{i\in \mathbb{N}} R a_i+Ra\) with \(a_i\in I\). Take \(J=I:(a)\). It follows that \(J\supsetneq I\). Thus \(J\) is countably generated too. We claim that \(I=(a_{i\in\mathbb{N}},Ja)\). It’s clear that \((a_{i\in\mathbb{N}},Ja)\subseteq I\). To show the reverse inclusion, choose any element \(c\in I\subsetneq I+(a)\). Then \(c=\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}} r_i a_i +ra\) with \(a_i\)’s contained in \(I\). Thus \(r\in J\). Consequently we obtain that \(I=(a_{i\in\mathbb{N}},Ja)\) which is a countably generated ideal. Contradiction.
